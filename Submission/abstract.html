BTrack & areas of exploration

Track 1 · Fan. Primary area: blade design for energy efficiency, air throw and low noise. Combined with: fan as a smart-home enabler (ambient temperature & humidity triggers).

Why both: a blade that changes shape with heat is only half a product. The motor controller must know the room's temperature and humidity — and know what the blade is already doing — or the two will over-react to the same heat. The same sensors then make the fan the room's climate node for the AC and exhaust fan.

·How we got here

Before choosing a solution we mapped where ceiling fans under-perform in Indian homes: 28 room/usage conditions (false ceilings, hot roofs, monsoon humidity, beds and mosquito nets under the fan, inverter power, …), each researched against ~600 sources, scored on a fixed rubric and stress-tested in a hostile review. The strongest finding drove everything in this abstract:

Fans are designed and rated at maximum speed in an open test chamber. They are used at low speed, at night, over a bed, while the room cools several °C and humidity climbs. A fixed blade and a fixed speed step cannot be right for both a 34 °C afternoon and a 25 °C dawn — so the user wakes up and reaches for the remote. We found no fan product or active patent that changes blade shape automatically with the room.

1The idea — what we are building and how it works

We are building a ceiling fan that adapts at three levels. Body: the outer 35 % of each blade carries a 25 %-chord trailing-edge flap turned by a helical thermostatic-bimetal coil that sits in the blade's own airflow. Above a 28 °C neutral point the flap deflects down (~0.8° per °C), adding camber and effective pitch so every revolution moves more air; below it the flap rises and the fan gives a softer, lower-power draft. Nothing on the rotor needs power — the blade is the thermostat, and it keeps working on a plain wall regulator. Reflex: a temperature/humidity sensor feeds firmware that converts T × RH into a target air speed at the person (ASHRAE-55 / Indian IMAC comfort models), then shapes RPM into a slow, natural "breeze", an inaudible sleep ramp, or a humid-flush pattern. Brain: an ESP32-C6 learns each household's comfort offset from their own remote overrides and shifts the Reflex target — fully offline, < 2 KB of RAM. The firmware infers the blade's current shape from motor current, so the mechanics and the software never double-react to the same heat.

Fig. 1 — The Body layer. The rigid front 75 % of the blade is unchanged and carries every primary load; only a light flap moves. Coil length 250 mm, strip 20 × 0.5 mm, high-flexivity bimetal.

3Why we believe this is new

  • A passively morphing ceiling-fan blade. Passive thermal pitch control exists only in an expired 1982 automotive engine-fan patent (US 4,487,551); fan patents we found change pitch only manually. Full search before Round 2.
  • Motor current as a blade-shape sensor. A morphed blade needs more torque at the same RPM; the driver's current-vs-RPM residual tells the firmware the flap angle for free — closing the loop between passive mechanics and active control.
  • Learning a comfort offset, not a schedule. "Speed 4 at 11 pm" learned in a heatwave misfires in the monsoon; "this user likes 1 °C cooler than the model" does not.

4Technical feasibility — how it works and why it is buildable

Body — the morphing blade (numbers we designed to)

Actuator choice. We compared eight smart materials. Thermostatic bimetal wins: linear, no functional hysteresis or fatigue, cheap, and proven for decades inside rotating automotive fan clutches. NiTi shape-memory wire with a steel bias spring is our backup (two-state "snap" version at the blade root); shape-memory polymers and liquid-crystal elastomers creep under centrifugal load, wax actuators weigh 50–120 g and take minutes, and "trained" two-way NiTi loses 30–60 % of its stroke over time.

Stroke. Coil rotation θ = k·L·ΔT / t: with specific curvature k ≈ 28 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ (high-flex grade, to confirm on datasheet), L = 250 mm, t = 0.5 mm → ≈ 0.8° per °C, ±8° over a ±10 °C swing. With flap effectiveness ≈ 0.5, that is ~4° effective angle change on the outer span — which, by r² weighting, produces ~60 % of the blade's thrust. Since flow scales roughly with √thrust, +10–15 % air delivery at the same RPM is a conservative blade-element estimate.

Loads. Dynamic pressure at the flap (r ≈ 0.52 m, 350 RPM) is ≈ 220 Pa, giving a hinge moment of ≈ 3 N·mm. Coil stiffness is ≈ 2 N·mm per degree (blocked torque ≈ 17 N·mm per 10 °C), and a 20 % overhang balance halves the air load → speed-induced flap error < 1°; the slight back-off at top speed acts as a natural limiter. Centrifugal pull on the 15 g flap is 10 N at 350 RPM and 16 N at the 1.25× overspeed test (438 RPM), taken axially by a thrust shoulder on a 3 mm stainless hinge pin rated well over 1 kN.

Thermal response. A 0.5 mm steel strip in 5–19 m/s airflow has a time constant of ≈ 10–30 s — the blade tracks the room, not the motor (the coil sits 0.44 m from the hub). Ceiling-level air is 1–2 °C warmer than at the bed; the neutral point is set by rotating the coil arbor (a calibration screw), not by changing parts.

Reflex — climate-tuned speed patterns

Air movement of ~1 m/s at the occupant offsets ≈ 2–3 °C of operative temperature (ASHRAE 55, SET method). A 16-row T × RH map (50–350 RPM) sets the base speed. Human studies show fluctuating airflow at 0.2–1 Hz with a 1/f ("natural wind") spectrum feels cooler than steady air at the same mean speed. The rotor's inertia (≈ 0.06 kg·m²) makes 0.5 Hz speed swings costly (~+28 % energy), so we split the job: firmware makes slow ±10–15 % swings over 10–30 s with jerk-limited S-curves (≤ 12 RPM/s, ≤ 10 % extra torque), and the blade wake supplies the fast gusts. Sleep holds a ≤ 150 RPM band and ramps at ≤ 3 RPM/s as the room cools. Humid-flush (≥ 32 °C and ≥ 70 % RH, where extra speed adds little evaporative cooling) uses a low base speed with a short burst every ~45 s.

Brain — tiny offline learning

Overrides are binned by half-hour × {awake, asleep} into a table of comfort offsets (°C) with Beta-Bayesian confidence. A change is adopted only if it repeats on two consecutive nights; one-offs decay. It runs in < 2 KB RAM and < 100 cycles per update on an ESP32-C6 (≈ ₹113 at volume, Wi-Fi 6/BLE/Thread → Matter). We rejected on-device neural networks (forgetting, flash wear) and bandit "exploration" (nobody wants an experiment at 2 am). A 24 GHz radar sees a sleeping person's breathing where PIR fails; lights-off marks bedtime. Cold start uses the physics map; seasons shifting the weekly mean temperature fall back to physics until new habits form; two disagreeing users default to the lower speed. Learned data is saved daily plus a brown-out-triggered save (~12 ms); everything stays on the device.

Safety-critical aspects. (1) No electronics, wiring or slip rings on the rotor. (2) Flap retained by pin + thrust shoulder designed for the 438 RPM overspeed case; hard stops at −6° / +10° prevent over-travel above 45 °C; a light torsion spring returns the flap to the stock (neutral) shape if a coil breaks — failure looks like a normal fan. (3) Three actuators matched as a set; blade-tip tracking ≤ 2 mm verified by strobe before every spin test; spin tests run behind a mesh guard. (4) Bimetal isolated from aluminium by POM bushes and nickel plating (monsoon galvanic corrosion). (5) A manual command always overrides automation within 50 ms; RPM limits are clamped in the driver; a watchdog falls back to the last manual speed. (6) Our prototype electronics are 24 V SELV — no team-built mains circuitry; the product path is a UART link to the fan's existing controller.

Component sourcing risk

  • Bimetal strip — Indian manufacturer (Shivalik Bimetal), but sample lead time may be 1–3 weeks. Fallback: coils from dial thermometers / automotive fan-clutch spares for the first static tests.
  • 24 V BLDC fan — must accept an external speed command. Fallback: off-the-shelf 24 V sensorless BLDC driver.
  • Custom-fabricated: flaps (3D-printed), coil winding + stress relief, trailing-edge slot machining — all in-house.
  • Imported: LD2410, ESP32-C6 modules — stocked by Indian distributors; nothing long-lead.

What we deliberately do not claim

Morphing does not give "free" air: by momentum theory +12 % flow costs ~+40 % ideal power whichever way you get it. The gain is who decides (the blade, passively), a lower-power gentle mode when cool, and — our hypothesis to test — less noise than buying the same air with tip speed (+12 % RPM ≈ +2.5 dB by V⁵ scaling).
