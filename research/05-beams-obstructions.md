# Ceiling Fan Aerodynamics Dossier — Condition 05: Exposed Beams and Ceiling Obstructions

*Prepared for Atomberg AtomQuest 2026. Scope: exposed concrete/wooden beams, ducts, or chandeliers close to a ceiling fan's inflow region in Indian residential settings.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Geometry**: A beam, duct, soffit, or hanging light fixture sits within or at the edge of the fan's inflow/return-air annulus — typically running across the ceiling at a depth of 150–450 mm below slab level and passing within 0–1.5×D of the blade tips (D = sweep diameter, 900–1400 mm, 1200 mm most common per Part A context).
- **Typical Indian occurrence**: Exposed RCC beams are common in older independent houses, duplexes, and budget apartments where false ceilings were not installed over the full room; wooden beams/trusses appear in hill-state and heritage construction (Kerala, Himachal); exposed ducting is more common in commercial/retail fit-outs and some modern "industrial look" interiors. No India-specific incidence survey was found in this search; frequency claim is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Driving constraint**: IS 374:2019 specifies that in the official test chamber, any projecting beam or ceiling structure that could interfere with airflow must be positioned no closer than 1 m above the fan-blade test plane (itself 3 m above ground) — i.e., the standard implicitly treats a sub-1 m beam proximity as a confound to be *eliminated* in test conditions, not a performance state it specifies pass/fail limits for in situ ([BIS IS 374:2019 PDF, Indian Fan Association](https://indianfanassociation.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/BIS-IS-374-2019-and-Ammendment-1.pdf)). This means there is **no BIS numeric limit for in-home beam clearance** — only a test-chamber exclusion rule. Field clearance recommendations (8–12 inch blade-to-ceiling minimum) come from manufacturer/installer guidance, not BIS ([SMA Fan clearance guide](https://smafan.com/blogs/smart-home/ceiling-fan-clearance-guide); [Top Ceiling Fans — mounting on exposed beams](https://topceilingfans.net/installation/mount-fan-exposed-beams/)).

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- A ceiling fan's recirculation loop relies on an **unobstructed, roughly axisymmetric return path**: air drawn down through the blades at the center, spreads radially near the floor, climbs the walls, and flows back along the ceiling toward the blade tips to replace the air being pumped down (the "inflow annulus"). A beam, duct, or chandelier sitting in this return path acts as a **partial baffle**, breaking the axisymmetric return flow into asymmetric lobes and shedding eddies off its edges — directly analogous to a bluff body in a wall-bounded recirculating flow.
- Mechanistically this is a mix of:
  - **Inflow starvation on the beam side** — the blade sector swept nearest the beam receives return air through a constricted, lower-velocity path, lowering local angle of attack /effective inflow velocity for that sector of the rotor disk (similar in principle to the "hidden ceiling fan" inhale-return problem studied by Lin & Hsieh, where improper housing/inlet geometry caused the fan to partially re-inhale its own downwash and measurably hurt power consumption and output; they found a minimum blockage distance of 80 mm and max ring-plate height of 30 mm improved performance — a cavity/obstruction-proximity study directly relevant to beam clearance, though for a hub-integrated housing rather than a free beam) ([Lin & Hsieh 2014, SAGE/Hindawi, via ResearchGate](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1155/2014/214967)).
  - **Cyclic/once-per-revolution (1P) loading** — as each blade sweeps past the beam's shadow zone, it briefly operates at different local inflow conditions than the rest of the rotation, producing a periodic aerodynamic load fluctuation at blade-pass frequency (BPF = RPM/60 × number of blades). This is the same physical mechanism documented broadly in fan-noise literature for tip-clearance/casing-proximity interactions, where discrete tonal noise at BPF is generated by unsteady interaction between the blade wake and a nearby solid surface, and tonal amplitude increases as the gap narrows ([tip-clearance axial fan noise study, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261673357_Experimental_and_Numerical_Investigation_of_the_Tip_Clearance_Noise_of_an_Axial_Fan); [BPF tonal noise reviews, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1270963821005939)). No published study was found that isolates a *ceiling beam* (rather than a fan's own casing/volute) as the obstructing surface, so the magnitude of beam-induced BPF tones in domestic ceiling fans is **[UNVERIFIED]** — the mechanism is well-established for analogous tip-clearance geometries, but the specific beam case has not been quantified in the literature surfaced here.
  - **Downstream eddy shedding into the room** — turbulence intensity near a rotating fan is already dominated by periodic BPF-linked fluctuations that decay with distance from the fan (confirmed by recent transient CFD validation work: velocity and turbulence intensity "exhibit periodic fluctuations associated with blade-passing frequency, which diminish with distance from the fan") ([Validated transient CFD study, J. Building Performance Simulation 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19401493.2026.2678829)). Adding a beam-generated eddy on top of this baseline would be expected to increase local turbulence intensity near the ceiling but the fraction attributable to the beam specifically is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Governing non-dimensional quantities** (standard framework, not condition-specific data found): clearance ratio (beam-tip gap)/D, beam-depth/D, Reynolds number at blade mid-chord (~10⁴–10⁵ for domestic fans), and Strouhal number for vortex shedding off the beam edge. No source located that reports measured Strouhal/clearance-ratio values for a ceiling-fan-plus-beam system specifically; treat any such number as **[UNVERIFIED]** until tested.
- **Quantified effects**: No peer-reviewed or BIS source was found giving a %-air-delivery-loss number for beam proximity in domestic ceiling fans. The only directly quantified obstruction-clearance numbers located are from the hidden-fan housing study (80 mm minimum blockage distance, 30 mm max ring height) ([Lin & Hsieh 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1155/2014/214967)) and a separate CFD note that "a small region below the ceiling fan where air is not driven downwards" exists "due to blockage caused by the motor of the fan" (i.e., even the fan's own hub creates a small obstruction wake) — context for scale, not a beam-specific number ([CFD ceiling fan simulation, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377576570_Simulation_Of_Air_Flow_Around_Ceiling_Fan_In_An_Enclosed_Space_By_Using_Cfd)).

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Higher RPM increases blade-pass frequency proportionally (BPF = RPM × blades / 60), which for a 3-blade fan at 300 RPM gives BPF ≈ 15 Hz, and at low (1–2) fan speeds roughly 5–8 Hz — these are sub-audible as discrete tones but the harmonics (2×, 3× BPF) land in the audible range and are the components typically flagged as annoying in BPF-noise literature ([general BPF-noise reviews](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1270963821005939)).
- Whether a beam obstruction creates a resonance or beat-frequency problem specific to Indian BLDC drive frequencies, or whether reverse/updraft mode mitigates the obstruction effect, was **not found in any source** in this search — **[UNVERIFIED]**, flagged as an open question for section 12.
- No evidence located on an "optimum speed band" for beam-adjacent mounting. **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked, qualitative — no condition-specific quantified ranking found)

Based on the physics in Section 2, the levers most likely to matter for this condition, in descending expected impact (engineering judgment, not sourced for this specific condition — mark as reasoning, not verified data):
1. **Downrod length / blade-to-ceiling clearance** — increasing clearance moves the rotor disk further from the beam's influence zone; this is the only lever with any sourced guidance (8–12 inch general clearance norms) ([SMA Fan clearance guide](https://smafan.com/blogs/smart-home/ceiling-fan-clearance-guide)).
2. **Blade count / chord distribution** — affects how concentrated the periodic loading spike is per revolution; not quantified for this condition in sources found.
3. **Hub/motor housing shape and inlet ring design** — the Lin & Hsieh study shows housing ring height and inlet-outlet area ratio materially affect inhale-return losses in a confined/obstructed inflow scenario ([Lin & Hsieh 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1155/2014/214967)).
4. Tip shape, winglets, pitch — **[UNVERIFIED]** for this specific condition; no source found.

### CFD approach guidance for this condition (from Section 2 of source study)
Recommended starting point based on the 2026 validation study: **Overset Mesh** method (best accuracy-to-cost ratio) with **SST k-ω** turbulence model, validated against experimental velocity/turbulence-intensity traces at multiple distances from the fan ([J. Building Performance Simulation 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19401493.2026.2678829)). For this condition specifically, the beam should be modeled as a fixed solid-wall boundary condition positioned at varying clearance/depth from the rotor-swept disk, with mesh refinement in the wake-shedding region behind the beam.

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No manufacturer in the current market (across the brands searched) markets any feature addressing beam/obstruction-adjacent mounting** — this itself is the headline gap. Whether that is because the effect is aerodynamically minor, or because it's an unaddressed niche, cannot be determined from available evidence — **[UNVERIFIED]**, worth a targeted CFD + wind-tunnel study (Section 10) before committing design effort.
- Candidate concept directions (engineering reasoning, not literature-backed for this specific condition — to be validated, not claims):
  1. **Asymmetric/adjustable downrod offset or tilt kit** that lets the fan's rotor disk be positioned slightly off the beam's shadow rather than centered — mechanism: reduces the fraction of the inflow annulus blocked by the beam; risk: may reintroduce near-wall asymmetric effects (see condition 07 brief); novelty vs. patents unknown pending full patent search.
  2. **Per-blade or inlet-ring geometry informed by the Lin & Hsieh hidden-fan findings** (80 mm minimum blockage distance, 30 mm ring height) adapted to a beam-adjacent inflow rather than housing inflow — needs validation, as the geometry is not identical.
  3. **Sensor-free, RPM/speed-adaptive logic that is not condition-specific but generally smooths 1P periodic loading** (if BLDC controllers already compensate torque ripple at other causes, extending this to beam-induced 1P loading may be a low-cost software lever) — purely speculative, no evidence found either way.
  Quantified benefit estimates cannot be given responsibly without primary measurement — doing so would violate the "never invent numbers" rule.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Real-room test**: mount a test fan under a simulated beam (adjustable depth 150–450 mm, adjustable tip clearance 0–1.5×D) in a room-scale rig. Instrumentation: hot-wire or vane anemometer grid at floor level (0.1 m), seated height (1.1 m), and ceiling-return zone; smoke/fog visualization to map the return-loop distortion qualitatively; sound meter positioned per IS 374 test geometry to capture BPF tonal content; power meter to check if blockage increases motor loading.
- **Grid**: at minimum, a radial grid (4–8 points around the rotor at 2–3 radii) is needed to detect the asymmetric sector effect described in Section 2, rather than a single-point velocity reading (which would miss the asymmetry entirely).
- **CFD plan**: follow the Overset Mesh + SST k-ω setup validated in the 2026 J. Building Performance Simulation study as the numerical baseline, then add the beam as a fixed-wall boundary at parametrized clearance/depth, and compare predicted velocity-field asymmetry and BPF tonal amplitude against the physical rig above ([source](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19401493.2026.2678829)).

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Exposed beams, ducts, and chandeliers near a ceiling fan's inflow region create an asymmetric partial obstruction of the recirculation return loop, producing (by analogy to well-studied tip-clearance and hidden-fan-housing literature) locally starved inflow, periodic once-per-revolution loading, and likely elevated blade-pass-frequency tonal noise — but no study located in this search has directly measured these effects for a *ceiling-beam* geometry specifically, and no BIS numeric clearance limit exists for in-home use (only a test-chamber exclusion rule). No current market product markets a beam/obstruction-specific feature, and the patent landscape search here was too shallow (3 marginally relevant results) to support any design-around conclusion — a dedicated patent-database pass is needed before product decisions.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. IS 374:2019's only beam-clearance number (≥1 m) is a *test-chamber exclusion rule*, not an in-home performance limit — don't mistake it for a design target ([BIS IS 374:2019](https://indianfanassociation.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/BIS-IS-374-2019-and-Ammendment-1.pdf)).
2. The closest quantified obstruction-clearance guidance (80 mm min blockage distance, 30 mm max ring height) comes from a *hidden/housed* fan study, not a free-beam scenario — transferability is unverified ([Lin & Hsieh 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1155/2014/214967)).
3. BPF tonal noise rises with decreasing clearance to a nearby solid surface in general fan literature — plausible mechanism for beam-induced noise, but not measured for this specific case.
4. No brand in the market search differentiates on this condition — an open field, but unverified whether that's because the effect is aerodynamically minor.
5. The 2026 validated CFD methodology (Overset Mesh + SST k-ω) is directly reusable as the simulation baseline for this condition.

**Open questions / not found**:
- Quantified % air-delivery loss or velocity-at-occupant-height change for a ceiling-beam obstruction specifically.
- Any India-specific prevalence data for exposed-beam ceiling fan installations.
- A properly structured patent-database search (this pass only ran general web searches, not Espacenet/WIPO/InPASS structured queries) — flagged as incomplete, not absent.
- Material/manufacturing considerations specific to this condition.
- Whether reverse/updraft mode or variable-speed control mitigates beam-obstruction effects.
