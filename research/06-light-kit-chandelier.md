# Condition 06 — Ceiling Fan with Integrated Light Kit or Chandelier

*Evidence dossier for AtomQuest 2026. Ceiling fan aerodynamics for Indian homes, condition: light kit / decorative chandelier mounted under or in place of the hub, or fan–chandelier hybrids.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Configurations in the market:**
  - **Underlight fans** (most common in India): an LED light ring/puck mounted directly below the motor hub, between the hub and the blades' rotation plane or just below it. Examples: Crompton "Blossom" (12 W LED underlight) [UNVERIFIED specific wattage per SKU, see §6], Orient Electric "Aeroslim"/"Spectra" with "3CCT underlight." ([Crompton underlight collection](https://www.crompton.co.in/collections/ceiling-fans/underlight-fans), [Orient Aeroslim](https://orientelectric.com/products/aeroslim-with-iot-remote-3cct-underlight-bldc-pro-smart-ceiling-fan), [Orient Spectra](https://www.gauryog.in/Orient-Electric-Spectra-Underlight-Pewter-Finish-Ceiling-Fan))
  - **Fan-light combo kits** (Western market pattern): a separate light fixture (globe, LED panel, or multi-arm chandelier fitting) bolted to the bottom of the fan's canopy/hub housing, below the blade sweep plane — e.g., Hunter Fan Company light kits, Big Ass Fans "Haiku L" integrated LED. ([Hunter light kit patents](https://patents.justia.com/patent/D875288), [Haiku L cutsheet](https://bigassfans.com/docs/haiku/haikul/cutsheet-haiku-l-en.pdf))
  - **True fan–chandelier hybrids**: a decorative multi-arm chandelier body with fan blades attached around or below it (common in luxury Indian/Middle-Eastern decor fans), where the chandelier itself sits in the blades' inflow path, not just below the downwash.
- **Geometry parameters that matter:** light-kit/chandelier diameter relative to hub diameter and to sweep diameter D; vertical offset between the light assembly and the blade plane; whether the obstruction sits **above** the blades (inflow side) or **below** them (downwash side); number/density of chandelier arms (open vs near-solid disk).
- **Prevalence in India:** Underlight ceiling fans are a fast-growing premium segment (BLDC + underlight combo is now a standard SKU across Crompton, Orient, Atomberg, Havells, Usha) driven by the shift to decorative/designer fans in urban apartments and the BLDC "silent + smart" positioning. No published BIS/BEE category-split data was found quantifying what fraction of Indian ceiling-fan sales are underlight models [UNVERIFIED — no authoritative market-share figure located]. Qualitatively, they are concentrated in urban/metro bedrooms and living rooms where the fan doubles as the primary light fixture (reducing clutter from a separate ceiling light), and in premium/builder-finish apartments. Decorative chandelier-fan hybrids remain a niche, high-end segment.

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Hub-blockage effect on the downwash core.** CFD and experimental studies on bare ceiling fans already show the motor/hub acts as a central bluff body: "CFD models have accurately predicted the small region below the ceiling fan where air is not driven downwards due to the blockage caused by the motor of the fan," and maximum downwash velocity occurs at the mid-radius of the blade span, with velocities "at the tip and near the rotor hub being very low compared to the mid-point." ([ScienceDirect, Transient 3D CFD modelling of ceiling fans](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360132317302743); [ResearchGate CFD study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317845483_Transient_three-dimensional_CFD_modelling_of_ceiling_fans))
- **Hub-to-sweep ratio threshold.** Literature on rotor/fan hub design indicates hub diameter is typically ~15% of wheel diameter; "when the diameter of the central hub is greater than 15% of the diameter of the cylindrical ring, a dead zone around the rotation axis is generated where air does not circulate because it is not reached by the blades." ([ResearchGate, Investigating Aerodynamic Performance of a Ceiling Fan](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299975586_Investigating_Aerodynamic_Performance_of_a_Ceiling_Fan)) An underlight puck or a chandelier body effectively **enlarges the aerodynamic hub diameter** beyond the motor casing alone, pushing the blockage ratio past this 15% threshold in many decorative designs, widening the pre-existing dead/low-velocity zone on the axis.
- **Where the obstruction sits changes the mechanism:**
  - If the light/chandelier sits **above or level with** the blade root (inflow side), it chokes the inflow boundary layer feeding the root section of the blades, analogous to the inflow-starvation mechanism described for recessed coves (condition 01) but caused by a solid decorative body instead of a cavity — expected to reduce effective blade root lift and add turbulence ingested into the blade root.
  - If it sits **below** the blades (true light-kit case, downwash side), it does not reduce blade inflow but instead sits directly in the core of the downward jet, acting like a secondary bluff body/diffuser that the already-weak hub-region flow must pass around. This is the mechanism patents such as the "ceiling fan airflow diffuser apparatus" explicitly target — a deliberately shaped diffuser/light-ring meant to **redistribute** (not block) this flow outward into the occupied zone instead of leaving a stagnant column. ([US12180976, Google/USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12180976))
- **Governing non-dimensional descriptor:** a hub/light blockage ratio, β = (effective obstruction diameter)/(blade sweep diameter D), analogous to the h/D clearance ratios used for ceiling clearance in other conditions. No standard published β threshold specific to light-kit obstructions (as opposed to plain motor hubs) was found [UNVERIFIED]; the 15% hub-ratio finding above is the closest quantified anchor in the literature.
- **Quantified effects:** No peer-reviewed or BIS/BEE-published number was found for the specific % air-delivery loss caused by adding an underlight ring or chandelier body to an otherwise identical fan [UNVERIFIED — flagged, not invented]. The qualitative direction (enlarged dead zone on axis, locally reduced downwash velocity near axis, little effect on tip-region/bulk delivery since BEE service-value testing per IS 374:2019 measures overall air delivery and power at a standard test rig) is supported by the hub-blockage literature above but the headline CMM figures from underlight commercial fans (e.g., 230–245 CMM, see §6) are in the same band as plain fans of the same sweep/RPM class, suggesting manufacturers compensate via blade/motor tuning rather than the underlight ring being aerodynamically negligible by itself [UNVERIFIED causal claim — correlation only].

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- **Stroboscopic effect is fundamentally an RPM/frequency phenomenon, not a thrust phenomenon.** Flicker/shadow strobing happens "whenever a ceiling fan blade crosses between a light source and your eyes," with the blade-pass frequency (RPM × number of blades / 60) determining the flicker rate perceived from shadows cast by blades moving across a nearby light source. ([Hi-Hyperlite guide](https://hi-hyperlite.com/blogs/comprehensive-guides/stop-ufo-lights-flickering-from-ceiling-fans); [Houzz discussion, "strobe light effect"](https://www.houzz.com/discussions/2599659/vaulted-lr-ceiling-combo-fan-light-the-strobe-light-effect))
- **Risk band for human perception/photosensitivity:** general flicker literature on photosensitive epilepsy puts the provocative stroboscopic range at roughly 3–70 Hz, with 15–20 Hz "of greatest concern," though susceptibility has been reported from ~5 Hz to ~84 Hz. ([Epilepsy Action](https://www.epilepsy.org.uk/info/seizure-triggers/photosensitive-epilepsy); [FlickerSense literature summary](https://www.flickersense.org/led-health-effects/flicker-below-100-hz-scientific-literature)) A typical Indian 3-blade fan at 300–350 RPM gives a blade-pass frequency of (300–350)/60 × 3 ≈ **15–17.5 Hz** — squarely inside the most provocative band when a nearby light source (not the fan's own integrated light, but a separate ceiling light, window, or the sun) is interrupted by the blades. This is a credible physical explanation for the "strobe light effect" widely reported by installers/users (Houzz thread above), but no formal clinical study tying Indian ceiling-fan RPM bands specifically to seizure incidence was found [UNVERIFIED — mechanism plausible from cited frequency ranges, outcome not directly studied].
- **Integrated underlight vs separate light kit matters for strobing:** because an *integrated* underlight rotates with (or is fixed concentric to, depending on design) the fan's hub and sits below the blade plane, the blades do not pass between the occupant's eye and the light source in the same geometric way a separate ceiling-mounted chandelier does — so integrated underlights are inherently less prone to self-induced strobe than a nearby separate chandelier/light fixture is. This is consistent with the industry response pattern cited below (double light assemblies above/below the blade plane) rather than a quantified study [reasoning from geometry + the cited design-solution pattern, not an independent measurement].
- **Variable speed (BLDC) / reverse mode:** no evidence found that reverse/updraft or "natural breeze" pulsed modes specifically mitigate or worsen the light-kit blockage or strobe effect; these modes address thermal stratification (condition 13) and perceived air movement, not the fixed-geometry obstruction discussed here. No direct literature link found [absence of evidence noted, not fabricated].

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked by expected impact for this condition)

| Rank | Lever | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hub/motor-canister + light-ring outer diameter** vs sweep D | Directly sets the blockage ratio β; literature's 15%-of-D threshold for dead-zone onset is the most load-bearing number for this condition. |
| 2 | **Vertical offset between light assembly and blade plane** | Determines whether the obstruction chokes inflow (above/at blade plane) or sits in the downwash core (below blade plane) — different fix strategies for each. |
| 3 | **Blade root twist/pitch and root chord** | Since the hub-adjacent region is already low-velocity, root-section aerodynamic shaping has the most leverage to recover flow lost to the enlarged blockage, rather than tip-region changes. |
| 4 | **Diffuser/light-ring shape (frustoconical, finned, perforated)** | Patented diffuser apparatus designs (US12180976) use ring/strut geometry specifically to redirect the stagnant column outward into the room rather than leaving it dead — a geometry lever independent of the blade design itself. |
| 5 | **Number of blades and blade-pass frequency** | Sets the stroboscopic flicker frequency; relevant primarily for the light/shadow-flicker sub-problem, not for bulk air delivery. |
| 6 | **Downrod length / overall hub height** | Secondary; changes how close the light/chandelier mass sits to the ceiling-return flow, same general lever as in conditions 01/03 but not specific to this one. |

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

- Underlight housings are typically **ABS/PC (polycarbonate) diffuser domes** for light transmission combined with an **aluminium or ABS structural hub/canister** carrying the LED PCB and driver — consistent with general decorative-fan construction claims (e.g., Crompton "Blossom" marketed as an "aerodynamic ABS body fan" with a 12 W LED underlight, per manufacturer collection page) [manufacturer marketing claim, not independently verified].
- Heat from the LED driver/PCB sits in the same hub region as the motor, so thermal design must manage **two heat sources (motor + LED driver) in one compact housing** — a materials/thermal constraint not present in a plain fan. No independent thermal-test data found for Indian underlight-fan hub temperatures [UNVERIFIED].
- Chandelier-hybrid bodies (glass/crystal arms, metal frames) add **rotating mass and off-axis weight distribution** if any decorative element is not perfectly axisymmetric, which couples into balancing/vibration requirements (see condition 28) — a manufacturing tolerance concern beyond a plain fan's blade-balancing spec, but no specific IS 374 clause for chandelier-hybrid balancing was located in the search results [UNVERIFIED; general IS 374:2019 covers "mechanical stability" without this detail confirmed].
- Manufacturing cost in INR: underlight BLDC fans retail from roughly ₹3,300–₹5,500+ in the Indian market based on listed Atomberg Renesa/Orient Aeroslim/Crompton Blossom prices found ([pricee.com Renesa listing](https://pricee.com/atomberg-renesa-ceiling-fan-price-in-india-113441); other listings above) — a retail price band, not a manufacturing cost breakdown; the incremental BOM cost specifically attributable to the light kit/driver vs a plain BLDC fan was not found in public sources [UNVERIFIED].

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No published independent quantification of air-delivery/service-value loss caused specifically by an underlight ring or chandelier body**, as distinct from a plain motor hub. Manufacturers publish only the finished product's CMM/W; there is no A/B (with-light vs without-light, same blade set) data in the public domain found in this search. **Gap size: potentially significant** given the hub-blockage physics (15%-of-D dead-zone threshold) but currently unquantified — a clear candidate for an AtomQuest experimental/CFD contribution.
- **Stroboscopic/flicker risk from fan-blade shadows is well documented qualitatively (user forums, lighting-industry guides) but not formally studied for Indian ceiling-fan RPM bands (300–380 RPM typical) interacting with Indian room lighting setups** (tube lights, LED panels, window glare). Gap: translate the generic 3–70 Hz photosensitivity literature into fan-specific RPM/blade-count guidance.
- **No chandelier-fan-hybrid-specific balancing/vibration standard** confirmed in IS 374:2019 search results — unclear whether off-axis decorative mass (crystal arms etc.) is explicitly covered by existing balance tolerance clauses. Gap: regulatory/compliance clarity needed, not just an engineering one.
- **Concept directions** (each needs validation, not yet proven):
  1. **Root-shaped "diffuser blade" design** — integrate the diffusing function (per US12180976 concept) into blade root camber/twist itself rather than a separate ring, potentially recovering hub-zone velocity without adding a part. Risk: patent overlap with US5795220A/US12180976 if too similar; needs design-around. Cost impact: minimal (blade tooling change only).
  2. **Decoupled light module mounted above the ceiling line (recessed) with a slim through-shaft fan below** — removes the light body from the aerodynamic flow path entirely. Risk: aesthetics/cost of a recessed mount; may conflict with condition 01 (recessed ceiling) trade-offs. Novelty: moderate; some recessed-mount products likely exist already [UNVERIFIED].
  3. **Asymmetric/segmented underlight ring with gaps aligned to blade root sections** — lets root-region air pass through gaps in the light ring instead of around a solid disk. Risk: thermal/LED-driver packaging gets harder with a segmented ring; needs light-output uniformity testing. Quantified benefit: unverified, needs CFD.
  4. **Anti-strobe light driver tied to RPM sensing** — use the fan's own BLDC RPM feedback to pulse/dim the integrated underlight at a rate that never aligns with blade-pass frequency harmonics, directly addressing the strobe mechanism described in §3. Novelty: plausible and leverages BLDC's existing RPM sensing; no patent found in this search blocking this specific RPM-synchronized anti-strobe control, but a dedicated patent search (§8 gap) should confirm before committing.
  5. **Separate up/down light assemblies straddling the blade plane** (as reportedly already used by some manufacturers to reduce strobe, per the Hi-Hyperlite guide) — lower novelty since this is already a known industry response; worth confirming via patent search whether it's claimed.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Instruments:** hot-wire or vane anemometer grid on a horizontal plane at occupant height (1.1 m seated, 0.3–0.6 m lying) plus a second grid immediately below the hub (within ~0.3–0.5 D) to directly capture the hub/light-kit dead-zone size; smoke/fog visualisation to make the stagnant column and any diffuser redirection visible; a lux meter / high-speed camera to quantify flicker frequency and shadow contrast for the stroboscopic sub-problem; sound meter and power meter per standard BEE/IS 374 test protocol for service-value comparison.
- **Test design:** same blade set, motor, RPM, and mounting height, with (a) no light kit, (b) stock underlight ring, (c) candidate diffuser-shaped ring — A/B/C comparison of air delivery (service value), near-hub velocity profile, and flicker frequency at fixed camera/eye position relative to a secondary room light source.
- **Grid points:** radial profile from axis to blade tip at 3–5 radii, at 2–3 heights below the fan, replicating the mid-span-peak/low-near-hub profile reported in the cited CFD literature, to confirm or refute whether a given light-kit design worsens or improves on the bare-hub baseline.
- **CFD comparison:** transient rotating-mesh (sliding mesh, not just MRF, per the methodology implied by the cited ScienceDirect study) simulation of the blade + motor hub + light-kit geometry, validated against the hot-wire grid above; compare predicted dead-zone radius against the ~15%-of-D threshold reported in the literature cited in §2.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary:** Adding an integrated light kit, underlight ring, or decorative chandelier body to a ceiling fan places an additional bluff-body obstruction in or near the fan's hub region — a region already shown by CFD literature to be the lowest-velocity part of the downwash, with a dead zone forming once the central obstruction exceeds roughly 15% of the sweep diameter. Beyond this hub-blockage aerodynamic effect, light kits introduce a second, independent physical problem: stroboscopic shadow flicker, where the fan's blade-pass frequency (typically 15–17.5 Hz for a 3-blade Indian fan at 300–350 RPM) falls inside the generally-cited 3–70 Hz photosensitivity risk band when blades interrupt a nearby separate light source. The Indian market (Atomberg, Crompton, Orient and others) has converged on integrated underlight rings as a mainstream premium SKU, and prior art (notably US5795220A and US12180976) already covers diffuser-ring geometries intended to redirect — rather than simply block — the hub-region downwash while also managing light direction to reduce strobe. However, no independent, publicly available quantitative test (CFD or experimental) was found that isolates the air-delivery/service-value penalty of the light kit itself, and the patent landscape search here is incomplete (Google Patents/USPTO only; InPASS, Espacenet, and Patentscope not yet queried), so no credibility can yet be assigned to a design-around's novelty.

**Top 5 insights:**
1. The hub-blockage dead-zone mechanism and its ~15%-of-D threshold are real, citable physics from existing bare-hub CFD studies — directly applicable, by extension, to light-kit/chandelier obstructions, even though no study has tested a light-kit hub specifically.
2. Stroboscopic flicker from blade shadows is a well-documented, independently-sourced user complaint and lighting-industry concern, with a plausible RPM-to-flicker-frequency link to the photosensitivity literature that has not been formally tested for ceiling fans.
3. At least two patents (US5795220A, US12180976) already claim diffuser-ring geometries that solve exactly this problem for airflow and strobe — any AtomQuest concept here must design around these, not duplicate them.
4. The Indian market already has multiple underlight BLDC SKUs in the 230–245 CMM / ~28–68 W range, but none publish an isolated light-kit-vs-no-light-kit air-delivery comparison — a clear, fillable evidence gap and a differentiator opportunity if Atomberg publishes one.
5. BLDC fans' existing RPM/current sensing could be repurposed for an RPM-synchronized anti-strobe light control — a plausible, currently-unclaimed (as far as this search found) smart-control concept worth a dedicated patent-clearance check.

**Open questions / what could not be found:**
- No quantified % air-delivery loss for light-kit/chandelier obstruction specifically (vs plain hub) — [UNVERIFIED], needs original CFD/experimental work.
- No IS 374:2019 clause specific to light-kit or chandelier balancing/safety was confirmed (only general "mechanical stability" language found).
- No Havells, Usha, Bajaj, Polycab, Luminous, Superfan, Gorilla, Panasonic, Minka-Aire, Modern Fan Co., or Dyson underlight/chandelier-specific model data was found in this pass.
- Patent search was limited to Google Patents/USPTO/Justia; InPASS, Espacenet, and WIPO Patentscope searches are outstanding and necessary before any design-around or novelty claim is finalized.
- No academic paper was found that specifically CFD-models or experimentally tests a ceiling fan with an integrated light kit/chandelier (as opposed to a plain hub) — this is the single biggest research gap for this condition.
