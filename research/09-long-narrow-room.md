# Ceiling Fan Aerodynamics in Long, Narrow Rooms and Corridors (Condition 09)

AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier — high aspect-ratio rooms (length/width > 2, e.g. 3 m × 8 m)

## 1. Condition definition

- **Geometry**: A room where length/width (aspect ratio, AR) exceeds ~2. Classic Indian examples: narrow bedrooms in redeveloped Mumbai chawls/apartments (3–3.3 m width is a common structural bay), corridors, galley kitchens, narrow verandas, railway-quarters-style rooms, and elongated living-dining strips in 1BHK/2BHK flats. A typical case cited in the brief is 3 m × 8 m (AR ≈ 2.67).
- **How common in India**: [UNVERIFIED] — no national survey quantifying the share of Indian rooms by aspect ratio was found in open sources during this research pass. Indirect evidence: Indian urban apartment structural bays are frequently constrained to 2.7–3.6 m width by RCC column spacing and Vastu/plot-width norms, while living/dining areas are often elongated to 6–9 m, which mechanically produces AR > 2 combinations routinely in redevelopment and budget-housing stock (builder floor plans commonly show 3.0–3.3 m wide bedrooms and elongated living-dining halls). No BIS/NBC document was found that defines or regulates room aspect ratio for fan placement. [UNVERIFIED]
- Standard single ceiling-fan sweeps (900–1400 mm, 1200 mm most common per IS 374:2019) were sized and tested for roughly square/rectangular rooms with AR close to 1; IS 374:2019 and BEE testing do not specify a room-shape-dependent air delivery figure — air delivery (210 m³/min minimum for 1200 mm sweep) is measured in a standard test chamber, not a long/narrow room, so published ratings do not capture aspect-ratio degradation. ## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Baseline fan flow field**: A ceiling fan produces a downward column (the "fan jet") roughly equal to or slightly larger than the blade sweep diameter D near the ceiling, which does not visibly widen until it reaches about 0.8 m above the floor, where it turns into a radial wall/floor jet spreading outward in all directions (near-axisymmetric in a square/centred room) [CBE/Berkeley ceiling-fan air-speed studies, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2v88v264 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232397431_Studying_the_features_of_air_flow_induced_by_a_room_ceiling-fan].
- **What changes in a long, narrow room**:
  - *Early wall impingement on the short walls*: because the near walls are only ~1.0–1.5 m from the fan centre (half of a 3 m width), the radially spreading floor jet hits the long side walls while still carrying high momentum, well before it has decayed the way it would in a larger/square room. This produces a wall-jet (Coandă-type) turn-up along the near walls, higher local turbulence and wall friction losses, and a reflected recirculation that competes with the outgoing jet [general wall-jet/radial-jet theory; https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/14/1/240 reports momentum flux in a radial wall jet can fall by up to 50% from friction/turbulent mixing as it travels along a ceiling/wall — the converse geometry (floor jet hitting a near wall) is dynamically analogous]. [UNVERIFIED — no study found measuring this specific floor→near-wall geometry in a fan context; physics is inferred from wall-jet theory, flag accordingly]
  - *Dead zones at the far ends*: in an elongated room, the fan (usually centred on the room's centroid, which is far from the short end-walls when AR is high) cannot project useful velocity all the way to the ends. Air speed decays roughly with distance from the jet axis per the CBE dimensionless model; beyond ~1.5–2×D from the fan axis along the long axis, induced air speed falls toward background/stagnant levels, producing the "dead zone" commonly reported anecdotally for narrow bedrooms with the fan only serving the bed directly below it while the far corners stay still. [UNVERIFIED numeric threshold — CBE tool applies to room width/fan-height ratios, not validated for AR>2 corridors in the source material reviewed here]
  - *Elliptical vs circular footprint*: because the fan's "coverage circle" is forced to intersect two close parallel walls, the usable (unobstructed) footprint is effectively clipped into a lens/elliptical shape rather than the full circle the fan would produce in open space — i.e., a large fraction of the fan's radial output is wasted hitting walls close to the axis rather than reaching occupants along the room's long axis.
  - *Recirculation loop asymmetry*: in a square room the return (wall-climb) flow is roughly symmetric on all four sides; in a long narrow room the return path up the two long walls is short and fast (high aspect of wall area close to the jet), while the return up the short end-walls is long and weak, starving the top-of-room entrainment near the ends and reinforcing the stratified, poorly-mixed zone there.
  - *Multiple smaller fans vs one large fan*: distributing coverage along the long axis with 2 (or more) smaller-sweep fans in series is the de-facto field solution in Indian narrow halls/corridors and is consistent with ASHRAE/CBE multi-fan spacing guidance for larger spaces (condition 08), though that guidance targets open halls, not specifically width-constrained corridors. [UNVERIFIED — no source found explicitly validating 2-fan placement for 3×8 m rooms with quantified uniformity data]
- **Governing phenomena to cite in the dossier**: inflow/radial-jet impingement on near walls (Coandă attachment), wall-jet momentum decay with distance and friction, floor-jet turning radius (~0.5 m impingement-zone radius reported at 2.3 m mounting height — see §2 physics sources above), recirculation-loop asymmetry, and jet decay with axial distance in the long direction.
- **First-order relations**:
  - Clearance/geometry ratios relevant here: wall-gap/D for the *near* (short) walls — in a 3 m room with a 1200 mm (1.2 m) fan centred, wall-gap ≈ 0.9 m, giving wall-gap/D ≈ 0.75, which is well inside the zone where wall interaction (condition 07 physics) applies on both sides simultaneously, unlike a single-wall-proximity case.
  - Room aspect ratio AR = L/W is the key non-dimensional parameter for this condition; no standard CFD/HVAC literature value was found for a critical AR above which single-fan uniformity collapses. [UNVERIFIED]
  - Jet/floor-jet momentum decay can be treated with standard turbulent wall-jet self-similarity scaling (velocity ∝ x^-1 to x^-1.1 for 2D wall jets; radial jets decay faster, ∝ x^-1 to x^-2 depending on confinement) — exact coefficients for ceiling-fan floor jets specifically were only reported for the near-fan impingement zone (0.5 m radius at 2.3 m height) in the sources found; far-field decay constants for this exact configuration were not located. [UNVERIFIED]
- **Quantified effects found in literature**: none of the papers located in this pass gave a numeric % air-delivery or velocity loss specifically for AR > 2 rooms. The only directly quantified figures found are general ceiling-fan ones (impingement radius 0.5 m at 2.3 m mounting height; jet not spreading beyond D until 0.8 m above floor; wall-jet momentum loss up to 50% from friction in a related wall-jet geometry) — all [carry over with caveat] rather than condition-specific numbers. Flag explicitly: **no peer-reviewed quantitative study of ceiling-fan performance in long/narrow (AR>2) rooms was found in this search pass** — this is a genuine literature gap, not an omission. ## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Increasing RPM increases jet momentum and should push the dead-zone boundary farther along the long axis, but it also increases the velocity/turbulence hitting the near walls sooner — i.e., in a narrow room, raising speed may worsen near-wall buffeting/noise before it meaningfully improves far-end coverage, because the limiting dimension (width) is reached first. [reasoned from jet-in-confinement physics; UNVERIFIED quantitatively]
- No resonance/beat-frequency or blade-pass noise issue specific to this condition (narrow room) was found; those issues are more associated with conditions 03/05/28 (long downrods, obstructions, imbalance). Not directly relevant here beyond generic blade-pass tone scaling with RPM.
- Variable-speed BLDC control and oscillation/reverse modes: no manufacturer or study was found describing an existing "narrow room" adaptive mode. [UNVERIFIED/GAP] A plausible unexplored concept (not evidenced): alternating/oscillating fan head or asymmetric blade pitch to bias throw along the long axis — see §9.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked for this condition)

1. **Sweep diameter relative to room width** — the dominant lever. A smaller sweep (900–1050 mm) reduces near-wall impingement severity (larger wall-gap/D) at the cost of total air delivery; IS 374:2019 minimum air delivery for 900 mm is 130 m³/min vs 210 m³/min for 1200 mm.
2. **Number/placement of fans along the long axis** — using two 900–1050 mm fans in series instead of one 1200 mm fan trades peak output for uniformity along the long axis (consistent with multi-fan spacing logic in condition 08, though not validated specifically for corridor widths).
3. **Blade twist/pitch distribution and tip design** — levers that change radial throw distance vs swirl could, in principle, be tuned to project farther along a chosen axis rather than issuing a symmetric radial jet, but no commercial product doing this for ceiling (non-oscillating) fans was identified.
4. **Downrod length / mounting height** — affects impingement-zone radius (found at 0.5 m for 2.3 m mounting height) and therefore how soon the floor jet reaches the near walls; shorter mounting (lower h) generally reduces floor-spread radius reach, which could reduce near-wall impingement severity but also reduces reach toward the room ends. Trade-off, not free win.
5. **Non-circular/elliptical or two-speed zone blade concepts** — speculative; no evidence base found (see §9).

No published ranking of these levers specific to AR>2 rooms was found; the ranking above is a reasoned synthesis from general ceiling-fan aerodynamics literature, not a cited finding. [UNVERIFIED ranking]

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **Gap**: No commercial ceiling fan or published study specifically addresses non-uniform coverage in AR>2 rooms; the field solution (two fans) is a system-level fix, not a product-level aerodynamic innovation. This is a genuine, evidenced-by-absence gap (confirmed across market scan §6 and literature scan §7).
- **Gap**: No validated CFD or lab dataset exists (found in this pass) for ceiling-fan floor-jet impingement specifically when two near (short) walls are both inside ~1–1.5×D of the fan axis simultaneously — the defining physical difference from condition 07 (single near wall). This should be the team's own CFD/lab contribution rather than a literature citation.

Concept directions (clearly labeled as team hypotheses, not evidenced claims):
1. **Biased/asymmetric blade pitch** — blades with differing pitch/chord on the long-axis-facing vs short-axis-facing sweep arc, to bias throw toward the room's long axis and reduce near-wall impingement intensity. Mechanism: non-axisymmetric thrust distribution. Expected benefit: [UNVERIFIED — would require CFD/lab estimate]. Risk: 1P imbalance/vibration (same family of issue as sloped-ceiling condition 04), cyclic loading, and complexity in balancing/manufacturing. Cost: moderate tooling complexity. Novelty vs patents: plausible white space, but overlaps directionally with US10989207B2's directional-output concept — recommend design-around via axial (not centrifugal) architecture.
2. **Two-fan "linked" system with a single controller optimizing combined uniformity** — not a blade innovation but a system/control innovation (synchronized speed/phase for two fans in a long room). Benefit: better uniformity than independent fans (unquantified). Risk: low aerodynamic novelty, more a UX/control feature. Cost: low (software/app only, reuses existing BLDC fans).
3. **Smaller-sweep, higher-RPM fan tuned for narrow-width** — deliberately undersized sweep (900–1050 mm) matched to room width with blade/airfoil re-optimized for the resulting lower h/D and wall-gap/D, instead of defaulting to 1200 mm. Benefit: reduced near-wall buffeting (plausible from wall-gap/D physics), at a BEE service-value trade-off since smaller sweeps have lower minimum service-value requirements under IS 374:2019. Risk: lower absolute air delivery for larger occupants/rooms within the same space. Cost: none beyond SKU proliferation.
4. **Elliptical/non-circular virtual footprint via adjustable/oscillating blade angle (low-speed mechanical sweep, not full head oscillation)** — speculative, no prior art directly found for a ceiling (non-table/non-pedestal) fan doing this; closest analogue is table-fan oscillation (not ceiling). High engineering/safety complexity (moving mass overhead); likely not worth pursuing given cost/risk vs benefit without more evidence.

All four directions carry **no quantified benefit estimate** from existing literature — any numbers used in the hackathon pitch should come from the team's own CFD run or bench test (see §10), not be asserted as established fact.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Scaled or real-room test rig**: Build/rent a room (or partition a lab) at a true AR≈2.5–3 (e.g., 3 m × 8 m, or a scaled 1:3 model at 1 m × 2.7 m with Reynolds-matched fan speed) with the fan centred on the short axis, offset along the long axis at a few positions.
- **Instruments**: Hot-wire or omnidirectional hot-sphere anemometer array (ASHRAE 55-style, since occupant comfort categories reference elevated air speed) at seated head height (1.1 m) and lying height (0.3–0.6 m); a vane-anemometer grid at floor level near both short end walls and at the room midline to capture the impingement vs dead-zone contrast; smoke/fog visualization for qualitative floor-jet-to-wall-jet transition; sound meter (dBA) near both near-walls (impingement-driven noise) and far ends; power meter for input W at each tested RPM.
- **Grid points**: At minimum, a line of velocity probes along the long axis at 0.5 m intervals from one end-wall to the other, at both 1.1 m and 0.4 m heights, plus a cross-axis line at the fan's own position to characterize near-wall impingement intensity.
- **Metrics to report**: mean air speed and turbulence intensity at each grid point vs distance from fan axis along both the long and short directions; ASHRAE 55 "elevated air speed" comfort compliance fraction of floor area; dBA; W; and a uniformity index (e.g., min/mean or std/mean velocity across the occupied grid) as the single comparative metric between single-fan and multi-fan configurations.
- **CFD comparison**: Model the true AR box (not a square proxy) with both short walls included in the domain; use rotating-frame (MRF) for a first pass and sliding mesh for final validation; k-ω SST turbulence closure (commonly reported in the ceiling-fan CFD literature reviewed in §7); validate against the lab grid data above before trusting CFD-only comparisons between design concepts.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Long, narrow Indian rooms (AR>2, e.g., 3×8 m) are common enough in redevelopment/budget housing that the condition is practically significant, but it is also the most under-researched of the "placement in room" conditions searched here: no peer-reviewed study, CFD paper, manufacturer feature, or patent was found that directly treats AR>2 ceiling-fan performance as its own subject. The physics can be reasoned from adjacent, better-documented phenomena — near-wall floor-jet impingement (condition 07's physics, but on both short walls simultaneously), wall-jet momentum decay (~up to 50% in analogous diffuser geometries), and the CBE jet-shape/impingement-radius data (0.5 m impingement radius at 2.3 m mounting height) — but every narrow-room-specific number in this report is inferred, not measured, and must be flagged as such to the hackathon team.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. The defining physical difference from a standard room is that *both* short walls sit within ~1–1.5×D of the fan axis simultaneously, doubling the near-wall-impingement problem rather than it occurring on one side only (as in condition 07).
2. The current, universal field "solution" is simply installing two or more standard fans along the long axis — there is no aerodynamically specialized product for this condition on the Indian market.
3. No IS 374:2019/BEE test condition captures aspect-ratio effects; published air-delivery and service-value numbers are measured in a standard test chamber and cannot be assumed to hold in AR>2 rooms.
4. The CBE Advanced Ceiling Fan Design Tool (dimensionless empirical model from 78 full-scale tests) is the single most relevant primary resource for quantifying this condition properly, but this research pass could not confirm whether its parameter set includes true aspect-ratio/corridor geometries — direct retrieval failed (DNS error fetching cbe.berkeley.edu) and should be redone by the team.
5. No patent was found that specifically claims a long/narrow-room-optimized or elliptical-footprint ceiling fan; the nearest analogues (directional/centrifugal ceiling fan US10989207B2, graduated-dihedral blade US7413410B2) represent moderate, not high, overlap risk for an axis-biased blade concept.

**Open questions / what could not be found in this pass**:
- No quantitative data on % air-delivery or comfort loss specifically for AR>2 rooms.
- No confirmation of how common AR>2 rooms actually are in Indian housing stock (no national survey located).
- The CBE Ceiling Fan Design Guide PDF could not be fetched (network/DNS failure) and should be re-attempted by the team directly.
- No InPASS (Indian Patent Office), Espacenet, or WIPO Patentscope search was performed in this session — only Google Patents, via indirect web search — so the patent table above is preliminary and should not be treated as a freedom-to-operate clearance.
- No sensor/smart-control product addressing room-shape adaptation was found; whether this is truly unexplored or just unreported in indexed sources is unknown.
