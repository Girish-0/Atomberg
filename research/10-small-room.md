# Small Rooms (Bathrooms, Kitchens, Study Rooms < 8 m²) — Ceiling Fan Aerodynamics Dossier

*Prepared for Atomberg "AtomQuest 2026." Research framework: Part A (common) + Part B condition brief 10 (small rooms, D/room-width > 0.4).*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Geometry**: A room where the fan's sweep diameter (D) is large relative to the room's smaller horizontal dimension (width W), i.e. D/W > 0.4, sometimes approaching 0.6–0.8 in very tight bathrooms or store-rooms. Typical Indian small-room floor areas: bathrooms 3–5 m², kitchens 5–8 m², study/utility rooms 6–8 m², pooja rooms 2–4 m².
- **Typical fan sizes sold for this segment in India**: 600 mm (~24"), 750 mm, and 900 mm sweep, versus the 1200 mm "default" size for bedrooms/living rooms. Industry sizing charts (Atomberg, Fybros, Orient) suggest: 600 mm for ≤50 ft² (~4.6 m²) rooms (bathrooms, balconies, pooja rooms, wardrobe nooks); 900 mm for 50–100 ft² (~4.6–9.3 m²) rooms (small bedrooms, kitchens, study rooms) [UNVERIFIED — vendor sizing guidance, not an independent standard] ([Atomberg sweep guide](https://atomberg.com/blogs/blog/ceiling-fan-size-chart), [Fybros guide](https://www.fybros.com/blog/ceiling-fan-sweep-size-guide-india)).
- **How common in India**: Very common — nearly every urban apartment and independent house has at least one bathroom, utility room, or small kitchen under 8 m². Market presence of dedicated 600 mm/750 mm SKUs from Atomberg, Havells, Crompton, and Orient indicates sustained demand, but exact unit-volume share by sweep size is [UNVERIFIED] — no public BEE/industry breakdown by sweep size was found in this search pass.
- **Note on IS 374:2019**: The standard sets safety, electrical, and (via BEE amendment) service-value requirements across sweep categories (900/1050/1200/1400 mm bands cited below), but it does not appear to mandate a minimum room-area-to-sweep ratio; room-size recommendations in the market are retailer/OEM guidance, not a BIS requirement [UNVERIFIED — could not locate an explicit clause in IS 374:2019 text during this pass; would need direct access to the BIS standard].

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Inflow starvation from all sides, not just above**: In a normal room, the fan draws return air from a roughly hemispherical volume well outside the blade tip radius. When D/W > 0.4, the walls sit close enough to the blade tips (wall-gap/D often < 0.3–0.5, same order as Condition 07) that the inflow boundary layer along the ceiling is choked on multiple sides simultaneously, not just one. The suction-side (upper-surface) pressure field that normally pulls air radially inward from an open ceiling plane instead pulls air from a volume already disturbed by the downwash that has rebounded off the floor and nearby walls.
- **Re-ingestion of own wake / short-circuiting**: Because the room is small, the floor-level jet spreads outward only a short distance before hitting a wall, turns upward (wall-climb, analogous to Condition 07's Coandă attachment but occurring on *all four* walls near-simultaneously), and re-enters the fan's own inflow zone at the ceiling before it has lost much of its downward momentum and before it has meaningfully mixed with room air. This is functionally a short-loop recirculation: the fan re-processes partially-used air rather than drawing fresh, quiescent air — reducing effective mixing (ventilation effectiveness) even though the local air-speed sensation under the fan may still feel strong.
- **CFD/experimental evidence of this recirculation structure**: Reported CFD work on ceiling fans in confined rooms shows a two-cell structure: a large-scale recirculation cell between the room centerline and the sidewalls, plus a smaller secondary recirculation cell trapped between the floor and the fan-induced downward jet; a broad band between the fan's radius and the walls shows very low, largely ineffective air speed, and this pattern does not improve by just running the fan faster [UNVERIFIED numeric values — the review summary did not give quantified % figures] ([ResearchGate CFD study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377576570_Simulation_Of_Air_Flow_Around_Ceiling_Fan_In_An_Enclosed_Space_By_Using_Cfd), [Academia.edu mirror](https://www.academia.edu/76765721/Simulation_Of_Air_Flow_Around_Ceiling_Fan_In_An_Enclosed_Space_By_Using_Cfd)).
- **Governing non-dimensional ratios**: 
  - D/W (sweep-to-room-width ratio) — the primary geometric driver of this condition; CBE/LBNL laboratory work explicitly uses fan-diameter-to-room-size ratio as a predictor, finding that the larger this ratio, the more *uniform* the air-speed distribution across the room (because the jet footprint covers nearly the whole floor) — but uniformity is not the same as adequate throw or good ventilation effectiveness, and a too-high ratio reintroduces the wall-choking/re-ingestion problem above ([CBE ceiling fan air-speed study, 5.5 x 5.5 m room with 1.5 m fan](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132321003838); [escholarship prediction model paper](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4p479663)).
  - Blade-to-ceiling clearance ratio (h/D) and wall-gap/D — same governing ratios as Conditions 01/02/07, but in a small room *every* wall-gap/D is simultaneously small.
  - Reynolds number at the blade: unaffected in principle by room size (depends on tip speed and chord), but practically, smaller-sweep fans (600–900 mm vs 1200 mm) run at a smaller blade chord and often higher RPM to compensate for lower tip speed at a given RPM, shifting the blade into a different Re regime — relevant to Section 3.
- **Quantified effects**: No peer-reviewed numbers for %-drop in air delivery specific to D/room-width > 0.4 were found in this pass; BEE service-value minimums differ by sweep band (Section 6) but these are measured in a standard open test chamber, not in a small enclosed room, so they do not directly characterize in-room performance under this condition — flag as a genuine research/measurement gap (see Section 9/10). ## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Small-sweep fans (600–900 mm) marketed in India run at notably higher no-load RPM than 1200 mm fans to partially compensate for the shorter blade radius and smaller swept area (e.g., Atomberg's 600 mm BLDC fan is specified at 840 RPM vs roughly 350–380 RPM typical for a 1200 mm fan) ([Atomberg 600 mm product/spec page](https://atomberg.com/600mm-ceiling-fan)). Higher RPM increases blade-pass frequency and tip Mach-regime noise sources, which matters more acoustically in a small reflective room (Section 2/5) because the listener/occupant is necessarily close to the fan.
- CFD/experimental literature on enclosed-room ceiling fans states that simply running the fan at a higher rotational rate does not reliably improve effective air circulation in the room because the dead/low-velocity band between the fan's radius and the walls persists regardless of speed — the limiting factor is geometry (recirculation-cell structure), not RPM [UNVERIFIED for exact magnitude, but directionally supported] ([ResearchGate CFD study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/377576570_Simulation_Of_Air_Flow_Around_Ceiling_Fan_In_An_Enclosed_Space_By_Using_Cfd)).
- No literature found specifically on reverse/updraft or "natural breeze" pulsed modes for this small-room condition; this is a gap. [UNVERIFIED]
- Resonance/beat-frequency risk: not specifically documented for small rooms in this search pass, but the combination of higher RPM (higher blade-pass frequency) and a small reverberant volume (fewer room modes, more audible discrete tones) plausibly raises the perceptibility of blade-pass tonal noise versus a large room — physically consistent with general room-acoustics principles (Section 5) but not confirmed by a fan-specific study. [UNVERIFIED]

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Ranked for this condition (most to least impactful, based on physics above and on how OEMs actually resize their small-sweep SKUs):

1. **Sweep diameter reduction itself** — the primary, already-adopted lever: going from 1200 mm to 900 mm or 600 mm directly reduces D/W back toward the ≤0.4 zone recommended by sizing guides, trading peak air delivery for appropriateness to the space. This is the dominant market response today (Section 6).
2. **Number of blades / blade count vs chord** — smaller-sweep fans often keep 3 blades but shorten chord proportionally; a case could be made for a 2-blade or wider-chord 3-blade design at small D to recover swept-area efficiency, but no specific Indian product confirming this was found. [UNVERIFIED]
3. **Pitch angle and twist** — higher pitch can partially compensate for reduced radius at the cost of higher torque/power draw; relevant to keeping BEE service value competitive at smaller sweeps (small-sweep fans have a *lower* minimum required service value band than 1200 mm fans under current BEE norms, see Section 6, which may reflect this inherent trade-off being accepted by the standard itself).
4. **Tip shape / clearance to wall** — rounded or raked tips may reduce the abruptness of the wall-impingement/re-ingestion loop described in Section 2, by shaping how the tip vortex sheds near the wall; theoretical, not confirmed by a small-room-specific study. [UNVERIFIED]
5. **Downrod length / blade-to-ceiling clearance** — secondary in small *rooms* (as opposed to small *room heights*, which is Condition 02); still relevant where small rooms also have low ceilings (bathrooms/utility rooms often do), compounding Condition 02's effects.
6. **Hub/motor housing size** — minor; smaller fans already use compact BLDC motor housings.

No literature was found that ranks these levers with quantified impact specifically for the D/room-width > 0.4 condition; the ranking above is a physics-informed inference, not a cited finding. [UNVERIFIED ranking]

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

- Small-sweep fans sold in India (600/750/900 mm) use the same material palette as larger fans — powder-coated aluminium blades (noted explicitly for Atomberg's 600 mm model, marketed for corrosion resistance, relevant to bathroom/kitchen humidity) ([Atomberg 600mm spec](https://atomberg.com/600mm-ceiling-fan)), ABS/engineering-plastic hubs and motor housings, and BLDC or shaded-pole/induction motors depending on price tier.

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **Gap 1 — No product addresses the re-ingestion/wall-choking recirculation physics directly; the entire market response is "just use a smaller fan."** Shrinking sweep reduces D/W but does not change the underlying wall-proximity flow physics (Section 2) — a 600 mm fan in a 3 m² bathroom can still have D/W ≈ 0.4–0.5. No product was found that changes blade/tip geometry specifically to manage near-wall re-ingestion. Gap size: potentially significant for comfort/ventilation-effectiveness in bathrooms and kitchens, but unquantified. [UNVERIFIED magnitude]
- **Gap 2 — No BEE/IS test protocol visibility into small-room (confined) performance.** Service value is measured in an open test chamber per sweep band (Section 6), so the star rating does not reflect in-room reality for D/W > 0.4 installations. This is a measurement/standards gap, not just a product gap.
- **Gap 3 — Acoustic design for small reflective rooms (bathrooms, tiled kitchens) is not called out as a distinct design target** by any manufacturer found in this pass, despite the plausible higher-RPM/higher-blade-pass-frequency tonal noise and the more reverberant small-room acoustic environment (Sections 2, 3, and general room-acoustics evidence in this pass — [AMCA fan-noise guide](https://www.amca.org/assets/resources/public/assets/uploads/FINAL-_AMCA_Fan_Noise_RG.pdf)).
- **Gap 4 — No data-driven answer to "below what room area should a ceiling fan be avoided in favor of a wall fan/exhaust fan"**; vendor sizing charts give rules of thumb (e.g., 600 mm down to ~50 ft²) but no lower bound or physics-based cutoff was found.

**Concept directions for an aerodynamics-led team:**

1. **Wall-aware tip geometry for small-sweep fans** — a tip profile (raked, serrated, or winglet, cf. Condition 07 levers) tuned specifically to reduce the sharpness of the wall-climb/re-ingestion loop at wall-gap/D < 0.3–0.4, the typical range for sub-900 mm fans in sub-8 m² rooms. Expected benefit: reduced re-ingestion of partially-used air, better real-world ventilation effectiveness (unquantified — needs CFD/lab validation). Risk: added blade manufacturing complexity for a low-price-sensitive SKU segment. Cost impact: likely small per-unit tooling change. Novelty vs patents: moderate — differentiate from US7665967-style small-diameter blade patents by focusing the innovation on tip/wall interaction rather than upper-surface camber.
2. **Variable/two-stage effective sweep** — a fan whose effective blade span can be mechanically or aerodynamically reduced further at very close wall-gaps (e.g., retractable or hinged tip segment), letting one SKU serve a range of small-room sizes while keeping D/W in a favorable band. Risk: mechanical complexity and reliability (moving tip parts), cost impact likely significant. Novelty: higher, but prior art in retractable/foldable fan blades should be checked (not covered in this pass).
3. **Low-noise small-sweep acoustic package** — pair the small-sweep high-RPM motor/blade combination with specific blade-pass-frequency detuning (asymmetric blade spacing, as sometimes used to break up tonal noise) targeted at the reverberant small-room environment. Expected benefit: lower perceived dB(A)/tonal annoyance in bathrooms/kitchens. Risk: possible small air-delivery penalty from asymmetric blade spacing. Cost: low-moderate.
4. **BLDC speed/current-sensing "confined-room" mode** — use motor current-draw signature (already available on BLDC controllers, cf. Condition 28/26 sensor approaches) to infer when airflow is being restricted by near-wall recirculation (characteristic back-pressure signature) and auto-adjust speed/mode. Speculative; no existing implementation found. Risk: signature may not be reliably distinguishable from other load changes (dust, voltage). 
5. **Dedicated small-room sizing/test standard proposal** — not a product, but a standards-influence play: propose an IS/BEE small-room (< 8 m², confined-wall) test configuration so star ratings mean something for this very common installation case. Low cost, high strategic/marketing value for a brand already leading on BLDC efficiency messaging (Atomberg), but outside pure aerodynamics scope.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Real-room test setup**: Select 2–3 representative small rooms (a ~3–4 m² bathroom, a ~6–8 m² kitchen, a ~6–8 m² study/bedroom), each with the fan mounted at standard height, and test at least two sweep sizes (e.g., 600 mm and 900 mm, or 900 mm vs 1200 mm) to span D/W ratios above and below 0.4.
- **Instruments**: vane or hot-wire/hot-sphere anemometer on a fixed grid (e.g., a 3×3 or 4×4 horizontal grid at occupant height — 1.1 m seated, 0.6 m near a counter/desk, 0.1–0.3 m near floor for kitchen/bathroom use cases) plus a grid near the walls to directly characterize the re-ingestion/wall-climb loop described in Section 2; smoke/fog visualization or a tuft grid near the walls and ceiling to visually confirm the two-cell recirculation structure reported in CFD literature; a sound level meter (dB(A)) at a fixed listening position relevant to each room type (near the mirror for a bathroom, near the stove/sink for a kitchen, at the desk for a study room) because acoustic performance is plausibly a distinguishing factor in this condition (Section 2/9); a power meter to log W and compute an in-room service value for comparison against the BEE open-chamber rating.
- **Metrics to report**: mean and spatial-uniformity (std. dev.) of air speed at occupant height; air speed specifically in the near-wall band (the "low-effectiveness zone" flagged by CFD literature) vs. under the fan; dB(A) at the listening position, at low/medium/high speed settings; measured service value (CMM/W) in-room vs. BEE-rated nameplate value, to quantify the gap identified in Section 9.
- **CFD comparison**: Model the same room geometries with a sliding-mesh or MRF rotor and a RANS (k-ω SST) or, if resources allow, a transient/LES approach to resolve the two recirculation cells reported in the literature (Section 7); validate against the smoke-visualization and anemometer-grid measurements above before trusting CFD-only predictions for any new blade/tip concept from Section 9.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Small Indian rooms (bathrooms, kitchens, study rooms under ~8 m²) routinely require a ceiling fan whose sweep is large relative to the room (D/room-width > 0.4), a condition the Indian market has addressed almost entirely by shrinking the fan (600–900 mm SKUs from Atomberg, Havells, Crompton, Orient) rather than by changing blade or tip aerodynamics. CFD literature on enclosed-room ceiling fans describes a two-cell recirculation structure — a large wall-to-centerline cell and a secondary floor-level cell — with a persistently low-effectiveness band between the blade tip radius and the walls that does not improve simply by running the fan faster; this means the standard market fix (a smaller fan) reduces the geometric ratio but does not directly resolve the underlying near-wall re-ingestion physics. BEE/IS star ratings, measured in an open test chamber, also do not certify how a fan performs once installed in this confined condition, leaving a real measurement gap between nameplate service value and lived comfort/ventilation effectiveness in these very common rooms.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. The market's only lever used so far for this condition is sweep-size reduction, not blade/tip aerodynamic redesign — leaving room for an aerodynamics-led differentiator (wall-aware tip geometry, Section 9).
2. CFD evidence shows recirculation-cell structure, not RPM, governs effectiveness in confined rooms — "turn it up" does not fix the physics.
3. BEE/IS service-value ratings are measured in open chambers and likely do not represent real small-room performance — a credible, cite-able standards/measurement gap.
4. Small rooms for this use case (bathrooms, kitchens) are also disproportionately humid/greasy, so the materials requirements from Conditions 16/20 compound with this condition in the same real installations.
5. Acoustic performance (higher RPM on small-sweep fans, in a small reverberant room) is a plausible but unverified differentiator nobody in the market is explicitly marketing against.

**Open questions / what could not be found**:
- No quantified %-drop-in-air-delivery or specific comfort-metric data for the D/room-width > 0.4 condition specifically (as distinct from general confined-room CFD).
- No confirmed IS 374:2019 clause on room-area-to-sweep guidance (only vendor rules of thumb).
- Patent landscape for this condition is thin/unverified and needs a dedicated CPC/assignee-based search (Section 8) before any design-around conclusion is drawn.
- No Indian-institution (IIT/IISc/CEPT) study specific to this condition was located.
- No confirmed existing sensor-based "confined room" adaptive control implementation.
