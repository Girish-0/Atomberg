# Ceiling Fan Aerodynamics in Open-Plan, L-Shaped and Double-Height Spaces (Condition 11)

*AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier. Prepared for Atomberg hackathon team. [UNVERIFIED] marks claims that could not be confirmed with a primary source in the time available.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Open-plan living/kitchen/dining (LKD):** one continuous volume, typically 25–60 m² combined, with no partition wall between the functional zones. Common in new apartment towers (2020s Indian metro developments) and in villas/independent houses with "great rooms."
- **L-shaped room:** a rectangular plan with a secondary wing offset from the main volume (e.g., a dining alcove off a living room), so a single centrally-mounted fan is geometrically off-centre relative to one or both wings.
- **Double-height / stairwell void:** a vertical opening (4–7 m, sometimes more) connecting two floors, often adjoining the fan's room with no wall on that side — common in duplexes, farmhouses, and atrium-style apartment lobbies.
- **Shared feature:** at least one side of the fan's circulation "cell" has no nearby wall to turn the floor-level outflow back upward (no wall-climb return path), or the return path is diluted across a much larger or taller volume than the fan was sized for.
- **Prevalence in India:** Open-plan LKD layouts are now a standard offering in mid-to-premium apartment projects in Mumbai, Bengaluru, Pune, NCR and Hyderabad (2 BHK/3 BHK "integrated living" plans), and in most independent/farmhouse construction with double-height entrance foyers. [UNVERIFIED — no single published statistic found quantifying the % of Indian housing stock with open-plan layouts; this is a design-trend observation from real-estate listings, not a verified primary-source figure.] Older housing stock (pre-2010 apartments, rural/independent houses) is predominantly cellular (walled rooms), so this condition is a growing share of *new* construction rather than the existing base.

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

**Baseline mechanism (confirmed from CBE Berkeley/ASHRAE-aligned literature):** A ceiling fan produces a downward jet that narrows slightly below the blades, impinges on the floor creating a stagnation point, then spreads radially outward along the floor as a **wall-jet / floor jet**. In an ideal centred-in-a-square-room case, this radial floor flow reaches the four walls, turns upward (Coandă-type wall attachment), rises along the walls, and returns along the ceiling toward the fan's inlet — closing a toroidal recirculation cell. ([CBE Berkeley fans guidebook](https://cbe-berkeley.gitbook.io/fans-guidebook/full-guidebook/ceiling-fan-installation-and-integration-with-hvac-system))

**What changes in open-plan / L-shaped / double-height conditions:**
- **Weak or absent return loop:** Where a side of the room has no wall within a reasonable radius (open archway to another zone, or a stair void), the radial floor jet keeps spreading rather than turning upward and returning. The toroidal cell cannot close on that side, so momentum that would have recirculated back through the fan's inlet is instead "lost" into the adjoining volume. This is consistent with the general wall-jet result that **wall confluent jets have slow velocity decay and can cover almost the whole floor precisely because the wall turns and sustains them** — remove the wall and the jet instead behaves closer to a **free (unconfined) jet**, whose centerline velocity decays as roughly x^(−1/3) with no wall-driven re-acceleration. ([ScienceDirect – low-speed non-isothermal wall jet](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0021863474900043); [jet decay literature synthesis](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/j/jet+centerline+velocity))
- **Breeze spreading thin over a larger footprint:** In open-plan LKD, the fan's rated coverage area (sized for a single room) is being asked to condition a combined floor area 2–4× larger. Published sizing guidance states that in open floor plans, **effective coverage can drop 30–40%** relative to the same fan's rated coverage in an enclosed room of equal area, because there is no wall to contain/redirect the spreading floor jet. [UNVERIFIED commercial source, directionally consistent with wall-jet physics] ([ceiling fan size chart discussion](https://whatsizeappliance.com/ceiling-fan/size-chart/))
- **Air escaping through a double-height void:** Where the open side is vertical (a stairwell or atrium) rather than horizontal, buoyancy-driven **stack effect** competes with/augments the fan's forced circulation. Stack effect moves air vertically through tall unobstructed shafts whenever there is a top-to-bottom temperature or density difference; in hot weather the flow in a stack tends to move downward, in cold weather upward. A fan-driven downwash next to an open stairwell can therefore either reinforce or fight the ambient stack flow depending on season/time of day, and some of the fan's downwash/return air is entrained into the vertical shaft instead of closing the room-level loop. ([Wikipedia – stack effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_effect); [stack effect building-physics explainer](https://aeroseal.com/blog/stack-effect/))
- **L-shaped geometry:** the fan (mounted centrally in the main rectangle) is necessarily off-axis relative to the secondary wing. The wing receives only the weak, decayed edge of the radial floor jet, producing a **dead zone with disproportionately lower velocity** than the main zone — the same asymmetric-coverage mechanism documented for furniture/wall blockage in long, narrow rooms, but driven here by missing *floor area* rather than a blocking wall.
- **Governing non-dimensional quantities:** the clearance/geometry ratios already used elsewhere in this condition set still apply (h/D ceiling clearance, wall-gap/D), but the operative one here is better framed as an **effective "wall distance to diameter" ratio, (distance to nearest return surface)/D** — the larger this ratio, the weaker the recirculation and the closer the flow behaves to a free jet. Buoyancy-vs-momentum competition at the stairwell interface is captured by a **Richardson/Archimedes number** comparing the fan's jet momentum flux to the buoyancy-driven stack flow, analogous to its use in the stratification condition (Condition 13) of this same study.
- **Quantified effects available:** the 30–40% open-plan coverage derating figure above; no peer-reviewed, India-specific quantified figure for velocity drop at occupant height in open-plan vs. enclosed rooms was found in this search pass — **[UNVERIFIED, flagged as a research gap]**.

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- No condition-specific published literature was found on RPM/frequency optimisation specifically for open-plan or double-height geometries. By extension from general fan theory: because the floor jet decays faster here (no wall reinforcement), a **higher sustained speed/RPM** is needed to project air to the same radius as in an enclosed room of equal floor area — i.e., the open-plan condition effectively shifts a fan's "adequate coverage" operating point to a higher speed step, at the cost of more power per unit area covered. **[UNVERIFIED — inferred from wall-jet physics, not from a direct test.]**
- BLDC variable-speed control is relevant here only insofar as it lets the user/installer compensate by raising speed; no literature found on resonance, beat-frequency, or stroboscopic issues being specific to this condition.
- Reverse/updraft mode: in the double-height/stairwell sub-case, reverse rotation (pulling air up and out radially along the ceiling) could in principle work *with* a downward (cold-weather) stack flow or *against* an upward (warm weather) one; no test data located. **[UNVERIFIED]**

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Ranked by expected relevance to "no return wall" / oversized-footprint conditions (reasoning-based ranking; no condition-specific test data found):

1. **Sweep diameter and multiple-fan layout** — the single biggest lever. Published guidance recommends **two or more mid-size fans (52–62", positioned per functional zone) rather than one oversized fan** for open-plan rooms over ~400 sq ft (37 m²): one large 72" fan produces a strong central downdraft but leaves zones away from centre (kitchen/dining in an LKD, or the far wing of an L-shape) with an estimated **40–50% less airflow**, whereas splitting into zone-matched fans was reported to give roughly **70% better coverage** for similar combined cost. **[UNVERIFIED commercial source, but mechanistically consistent with wall-jet decay]** ([Big Ass Fans-style sizing discussion](https://whatsizeappliance.com/ceiling-fan/size-chart/))
2. **Downrod length / mounting height** — in double-height spaces, a longer downrod to bring the fan closer to occupant height recovers jet momentum at the floor that would otherwise decay over an unusually tall throw (shared mechanism with Condition 03, High ceilings).
2. **Blade pitch/chord and tip design** — higher pitch (≥12–13° cited for large open-plan-suited fans) and larger chord increase thrust per RPM, partially compensating for the lack of wall-assisted recirculation. [UNVERIFIED commercial spec, not an independent test]
4. **Number of blades / motor torque (BLDC vs induction)** — BLDC's higher available torque at low RPM supports running larger-sweep fans efficiently; relevant to compensating for reduced effective coverage, but no direct open-plan test data found.
5. **Canopy/hub shape, winglets** — likely secondary for this specific condition; no literature found tying tip geometry to open-plan performance specifically.

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

General material trade-offs (aluminium vs ABS/ASA/PP/glass-filled nylon blades, stamping vs injection moulding, balancing tolerances, cost in INR) are not differentially affected by "open-plan" geometry itself — they matter the same way as in a standard installation. The only condition-linked implication: if the response to open-plan/double-height rooms is **multiple smaller fans instead of one large fan** (per Section 4), the per-unit manufacturing economics shift toward higher volumes of mid-size (1200 mm) models rather than large (1400 mm+) ones, which is the segment Indian OEMs already manufacture at scale. **[Inference, not sourced]**

## 7. Academic and industry research

- **CBE Berkeley / UC Berkeley Center for the Built Environment** — the primary credible source located. Their **Ceiling Fan Guidebook** documents the floor-jet-impingement-and-wall-return mechanism in detail, and their **Ceiling-Fan-Integrated Air Conditioning (CFIAC)** research (with Chen et al., tested experimentally at CBE following the same geometry as Chen et al. 2020) studies how a side-wall supply jet interacts with a ceiling fan — directly relevant physics (a jet entering a room from one side, analogous to an open archway) though the published studies are framed around AC integration rather than open-plan architecture per se. ([CBE fans guidebook](https://cbe-berkeley.gitbook.io/fans-guidebook/full-guidebook/ceiling-fan-installation-and-integration-with-hvac-system); [ResearchGate: Ceiling-Fan-Integrated AC — airflow and temperature characteristics](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338525067_Ceiling-Fan-Integrated_Air_Conditioning_airflow_and_temperature_characteristics_of_a_sidewall-supply_jet_interacting_with_a_ceiling_fan); [ResearchGate: thermal comfort evaluations](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357283379_Ceiling-fan-integrated_air-conditioning_thermal_comfort_evaluations))
- **Jayasree, Jinshah & Srinivas (2022), "The effect of opening windows on the airflow distribution inside naturally ventilated residential bedrooms with ceiling fans"**, published in a SAGE journal — studied non-uniformity of fan-driven air velocity with and without open windows; found opening a window reduced velocity non-uniformity from 76% to 39% in larger rooms. This is the closest **India-relevant, peer-reviewed-adjacent** study found that deals with a fan room having an "opening" to outside, analogous in principle to an opening to an adjoining open-plan zone, though the study is about window openings, not architectural open-plan layouts. Full methodology/mesh/instrumentation not verified beyond the abstract in this pass. ([SAGE journal abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/01436244211024084)) **[Abstract-level only; full text not read — mark quantitative figures as reported-by-abstract, not independently confirmed]**
- **Free-jet / wall-jet decay literature** (general fluid mechanics, not ceiling-fan-specific): establishes that a confined/wall jet decays slower than a free jet, and that wall-confluent jets can cover a full room floor with acceptable draught per ISO 7730 — the physical basis for why removing the wall (open plan) is expected to hurt coverage. ([ScienceDirect – wall jet](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0021863474900043); general free-jet decay synthesis via [science.gov](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/j/jet+centerline+velocity))
- **No IIT/IISc/CEPT-specific published study on ceiling fans in open-plan/L-shaped/double-height Indian residential geometries was located in this search pass.** This is a notable literature gap, not a confirmed absence — a more exhaustive, full-text academic database search (Scopus/Web of Science, not available via general web search here) is recommended before concluding no such study exists. **[Gap — flagged, not verified as a true absence]**
- **CFD approach:** no condition-specific CFD methodology paper found. By extension from general ceiling-fan CFD practice (not confirmed condition-specific), a Multiple Reference Frame (MRF) or sliding-mesh rotating-blade model with a k-ω SST or similar turbulence closure, validated against PIV/hot-wire measurements, would be the standard approach; modelling an open-plan case specifically requires extending the domain to the full combined volume (including the second zone or the stairwell void) rather than truncating at a notional "room boundary," since the whole point of this condition is that there is no boundary there.

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No product in the surveyed Indian or global catalogues explicitly markets a feature for open-plan, L-shaped, or double-height-void rooms.** Every spec sheet found addresses sweep/power/star-rating for a generic room, not a geometry-aware claim. This is a genuine white space, but its size is **[UNVERIFIED]** — no market-sizing data on the share of Indian homes with open-plan layouts was found.
- **Gap 1 — No "zone-aware" multi-fan coordination product for Indian open-plan homes.** Big Ass Fans-style zoned sizing guidance exists for the US market but no equivalent synchronised/app-coordinated multi-fan Indian BLDC product was found. *Concept:* two or more BLDC fans in one open-plan LKD, app/remote-synchronised (shared speed profile, optionally counter-rotating to create a push-pull loop across the open boundary instead of relying on a wall). Expected benefit: closing an artificial "virtual wall" recirculation loop between two fans instead of each one leaking into the other's zone — quantified benefit **not available, would need testing** (Section 10). Risk: added BOM/complexity (wireless sync module), consumer confusion over why two fans are "needed." Novelty vs. patents: likely novel in the synchronisation logic even where individual-fan patents exist (Section 8), since none of the found patents address inter-fan coordination.
- **Gap 2 — No directional/asymmetric-thrust fan for L-shaped rooms.** *Concept:* asymmetric blade pitch or trailing-edge geometry that biases floor-jet spread toward the off-axis wing of an L-shaped room, installer-configurable at setup (orient the fan's "strong side" toward the wing). Expected benefit: reduce the dead-zone velocity deficit in the wing — magnitude unverified. Risk: added manufacturing complexity (non-identical blades or per-blade pitch) and balance/vibration risk if asymmetry is overdone. Overlaps partially with US7726945B2 and CN2145274Y (Section 8); a design-around would favour a fixed asymmetric blade set over a mechanically adjustable one.
- **Gap 3 — No stack-effect-aware control for fans adjoining a double-height void.** *Concept:* a temperature-differential sensor (ceiling vs. floor, or ceiling vs. a sensor near the void) driving automatic reverse/forward mode and speed, so the fan works with rather than against the seasonal stack flow direction. Expected benefit: reduces the "air escaping upward and not returning" loss specifically in the hot-stack-flow-downward season. Risk: sensor cost, calibration complexity, and the fact that stack effect magnitude in a typical 2-storey Indian home void may be small relative to fan-driven flow — **unverified without a field measurement.**
- **Gap 4 — No published India-specific quantified study of velocity/comfort loss in open-plan vs. enclosed rooms.** This is as much a research gap as a product gap: before committing to any of the above concepts, the team would need its own measurement (Section 10) because the only supporting figures found (30–40% coverage drop, 40–50% airflow loss at zone edges, 70% coverage improvement from zoning) are **commercial/consumer-guide sourced, not independently tested**, and are US-market, not India-specific.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Room setup:** build/instrument two comparable test spaces — one enclosed rectangular room, one with an open archway or missing wall of controlled width (0%, 25%, 50%, 100% of one wall open) to isolate the effect of "missing return wall" as a single variable. Separately, a double-height/stairwell mock-up or an accessible real duplex stairwell for the buoyancy sub-case.
- **Instruments:** 
  - Hot-wire or omnidirectional hot-sphere anemometer grid at occupant height (0.3–0.6 m lying, 1.1 m seated) on a fixed XY grid spanning the room and the "open" zone beyond the missing wall, to map how far the usable breeze actually extends vs. the enclosed-room baseline.
  - Smoke/fog visualisation to directly observe whether the return loop closes on the open side or escapes.
  - Thermocouple array (floor-to-ceiling profile, plus one near the stairwell opening) to quantify any stack-effect temperature differential driving buoyant flow.
  - Sound meter and power meter at each tested speed step, to check whether higher-speed compensation (Section 3) costs disproportionate power/noise.
- **Metrics to report:** mean and uniformity (e.g., coefficient of variation, or the %-non-uniformity metric used in the Jayasree et al. 2022 study) of velocity at occupant height, extending the measurement grid progressively further from the fan into the open zone; coverage radius at a defined minimum comfort velocity (e.g., 0.3–0.5 m/s per ASHRAE 55 elevated-air-speed guidance — note ASHRAE 55 itself must be checked directly for the exact threshold used, not assumed here); power and dB(A) at each tested speed.
- **CFD validation targets:** model the same open-wall-fraction sweep (0/25/50/100%) and the stairwell case in CFD (MRF or sliding mesh, k-ω SST or equivalent), and compare predicted velocity-decay-with-distance curves against the measured hot-wire grid — the acceptance criterion should be reproducing both the **magnitude** and the **location** of the dead zone in the L-shaped/open case, not just an overall average.

## 11. Sensors and smart control (if relevant)

- **Relevant here**, via the stack-effect concept in Section 9 (Gap 3): a ceiling-vs-floor or ceiling-vs-void temperature sensor could let a BLDC fan infer whether ambient buoyancy is currently helping or opposing its downwash near an open stairwell, and adjust forward/reverse mode and speed accordingly. **No existing commercial implementation of this specific logic was found** in this search pass — distinct from the general reverse/updraft-mode feature already common on BLDC fans (which is user-selected, not condition-sensing). **[Gap, not a confirmed existing feature]**
- Occupancy/room-shape sensing (e.g., an installer-time app step where the user indicates "this wall is open" or "there is a stairwell on this side") is a low-cost, non-exotic way to let a single fan pre-bias an asymmetric-thrust design (Gap 2) without needing continuous real-time sensing — a software/configuration lever rather than new hardware.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary:** Open-plan, L-shaped and double-height Indian living spaces remove or weaken the wall that a ceiling fan's recirculation loop depends on to close; physics from CBE Berkeley's wall-jet/floor-jet research and general free-jet decay theory indicates this should measurably widen dead zones and reduce effective coverage (commercial sizing guides, not independently verified, suggest a 30–50% coverage penalty), yet no surveyed Indian or global ceiling-fan manufacturer (Atomberg, Crompton, Havells, Orient, Usha, Bajaj, Polycab, Luminous, Gorilla, Panasonic, Big Ass Fans, Hunter, Minka-Aire, Dyson) markets a feature explicitly designed for this condition, and no India-specific peer-reviewed quantification of the effect was located. This is therefore a credible, currently unaddressed opportunity area, but one where the team's first move should be its own grid-anemometer measurement (Section 10) before committing engineering effort, since every supporting number in this report is either a manufacturer claim or a consumer-guide figure rather than an independently verified test result.

**Top 5 insights (ranked):**
1. The core mechanism is well-established fluid mechanics (wall-jet vs. free-jet decay) even though no one has published it specifically for Indian open-plan ceiling-fan use — the physics transfer is low-risk, the quantification is not yet done.
2. Multi-fan zoning (not a single oversized fan) is the only concrete, evidence-backed mitigation found in the market, and it is a US/commercial-fan-industry practice, not yet common in Indian BLDC residential products.
3. No patent was found that squarely covers "synchronised multi-fan zoning" or "stack-effect-aware reverse mode" — these look like the least crowded concept directions among those considered.
4. The L-shaped-room dead zone and the double-height stack-effect interaction are mechanistically different problems (missing horizontal wall vs. vertical buoyancy competition) and likely need different solutions — treating "open-plan" as one condition risks conflating them.
5. The single biggest research gap is the absence of any India-specific quantified field or CFD study — this report's physics is sound but its numbers are largely [UNVERIFIED] commercial claims, so the team's own measurement plan (Section 10) is not optional groundwork, it is the prerequisite for any concept-level claim.

**Open questions / what could not be found:**
- No India-specific percentage of homes with open-plan layouts.
- No peer-reviewed, full-text study isolating "missing wall" as a variable for ceiling-fan coverage (the Jayasree et al. 2022 study is the closest analogue, window-opening not open-plan architecture, and was reviewed at abstract level only).
- No verified BEE/BIS-registered test data (only manufacturer claims) for any model listed in Section 6.
- Patent landscape search in Section 8 fell short of the 10–20 family target and should be re-run with dedicated patent-database tools (Espacenet, InPASS, WIPO Patentscope) rather than general web search.
- No existing product implementation found for stack-effect-aware automatic mode switching — absence noted but not proven exhaustive.
