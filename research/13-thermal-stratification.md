# Thermal Stratification (Hot Air Pooled at the Ceiling) — AtomQuest 2026 Research Dossier

## 1. Condition definition

Thermal stratification is the vertical layering of air temperature in a room, with warmer, lower-density air collecting near the ceiling and cooler, denser air settling near the floor. It is driven by natural buoyancy: any heat source (solar-heated roof slab, tin/asbestos roof, lighting, people, appliances) warms air that rises and accumulates under the ceiling because it cannot escape upward through a solid roof ([Wikipedia: Thermal destratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_destratification)).

- **Magnitude in unmixed rooms:** Documented gradients without active mixing are roughly **0.7 °C/m** (about 2 °C floor-to-ceiling in a normal room), and up to several °C/m in large/high-ceilinged or hot-roof spaces. Fan-driven destratification has been measured to cut this to about **0.3 °C/m**, less than half the unmixed gradient ([Performance Comparison of Destratification Fans for Large Spaces, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304713559_Performance_Comparison_of_Destratification_Fans_for_Large_Spaces)).
- **Warehouse-scale field data:** Vertical gradients up to **3.0 °C** over a year of monitoring, with a maximum daily-average gradient of **8.5 °F (~4.7 °C)** recorded in some field tests ([Purdue e-Pubs IHPBC](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1251&context=ihpbc); [ASHRAE Journal — HVLS Fans and Airplane Hangar Destratification](https://www.ashrae.org/technical-resources/ashrae-journal/featured-articles/impact-of-hvls-fans-on-airplane-hangar-air-destratification)).
- **India-specific roof/ceiling temperatures:** In Delhi summer conditions (ambient ~42 °C, solar radiation ~900 W/m²), an untreated RCC roof slab (150 mm) surface reaches **65–70 °C**; the ceiling (slab underside) runs **4–7 °C cooler than the roof surface**, and indoor room temperature is typically **2–4 °C below ceiling temperature** depending on ventilation and wall insulation — i.e., overall roof-to-room differentials of **6–8 °C are common on hot top floors** [UNVERIFIED precise India-specific stratification-gradient figures below ceiling plane, since most cited sources are generic/US/warehouse studies] ([Sterling Technotrade — Heat Reflective Roof Coating](https://sterlingtechnotrade.com/blog/heat-reflective-roof-coating-india); [Houzz India — Keeping the Roof Cool](https://www.houzz.in/magazine/how-to-beat-the-summer-heat-by-keeping-the-roof-cool-stsetivw-vs~120620519)).
- **Prevalence in India:** Top-floor flats under RCC slabs or tin/asbestos sheet roofs (common in independent houses, rural homes, and the top floor of walk-up apartments) are the dominant case; this condition overlaps heavily with Condition 15 (hot-roof top-floor rooms) in this same brief set. No single BEE/ISHRAE prevalence statistic for "degree of stratification in Indian homes" was found in this pass — flagged **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Inflow starvation of hot air:** The fan's actuator-disk inflow draws from the volume immediately above the blades — in a stratified room that volume is dominated by the hottest, least dense air sitting at the ceiling. In normal (summer/cooling) rotation the fan entrains this hot layer and drives it downward as the core of the downwash jet, so the air reaching occupant height is warmer than the room average unless the jet has enough throw and entrainment to properly mix with cooler floor-level air en route ([Thermal destratification, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_destratification)).
- **Buoyancy vs. momentum — Richardson/Archimedes number:** The competition between the fan's downward momentum flux and buoyant restoring force is captured by a Richardson number, Ri = g·ΔT·L / (T·V²) (or equivalently an Archimedes number for the jet), where ΔT is the local vertical temperature difference, L a characteristic length (e.g., jet throw or room height), T absolute temperature, and V a characteristic jet velocity. When Ri is large (weak jet, strong gradient), the downwash jet loses momentum before reaching the floor and the hot ceiling layer "slumps" back upward, re-stratifying; when Ri is small (strong jet, weak gradient) the jet fully penetrates and mixes the column. Richardson-number-based mixing analysis is an established framework for stratified-tank and stratified-room mixing problems ([Richardson's number and hot-water tanks, OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1808398)).
- **Downwash jet decay and re-stratification:** As the jet descends it entrains ambient air and decelerates (a round turbulent jet's centerline velocity decays roughly as 1/distance beyond the "potential core"). In a stratified room, the entrained ambient air itself has a temperature gradient, so the jet mixes hot air into itself as it falls — occupants under the fan feel air warmer than floor-level ambient, not cooler, if the room is only being recirculated rather than actually destratified.
- **Destratification vs. recirculation — the central open question this condition brief must resolve:** Research explicitly distinguishes whether a ceiling fan "destratifies" (permanently flattens the vertical gradient by sustained mixing) or merely "recirculates" hot air onto occupants without reducing the overall gradient. Validated field/CFD studies on large spaces show fans measurably flatten the gradient (0.7→0.3 °C/m) when run continuously and sized/placed correctly ([ResearchGate, Performance Comparison of Destratification Fans](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304713559_Performance_Comparison_of_Destratification_Fans_for_Large_Spaces)), but a Harvard thesis found that **continuous, variable-speed forward-direction operation can destratify more efficiently (lower energy) than running in reverse ("leading paddle" mode)** — reverse mode is not automatically better and can waste energy and cause draft complaints ([Comparing Modes of Operation for Residential Ceiling Fans, Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-ec98-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)).
- **CFD modelling approaches validated for this condition:** Multiple Reference Frame (MRF) models for rotating ceiling/paddle fans, and Pressure-Jump actuator-disk models for axial "bucket"-type destratification fans, both validated against warehouse field data; buoyancy is modelled via the Boussinesq approximation in these studies ([Numerical and Experimental Study of Thermal Stratification in Large Warehouses, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/211519163.pdf); [ScienceDirect — thermal destratification for large warehouse energy savings](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378778817307442)).
- **Quantified destratification-cooling effect (warehouse CFD):** Destratification was shown to reduce ceiling-level temperature by up to **4.0 °C in summer** and raise floor-level temperature by up to **7.0 °C in winter heating mode**, i.e. it works in both directions by flattening the profile toward the mean ([ScienceDirect — Experimental and numerical investigation of vertical temperature gradients in warehouses](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378778825001860)).
- **Comfort-model relevance (not pure physics but governs the "so what"):** ASHRAE 55's elevated-air-speed provision credits air movement above 0.20 m/s with raising the acceptable operative temperature; up to **0.8 m/s** is allowed without occupant control (≈3 K comfort-temperature offset), and up to **1.2 m/s** with occupant control ([SimScale — What Is ASHRAE 55?](https://www.simscale.com/blog/ashrae-55-thermal-comfort/); [ASHRAE Journal Addendum h to 55-2020](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/55_2020_h_20221230.pdf)). This matters for the stratified-room case because the comfort benefit is a trade between air speed and the actual temperature of the air being delivered — if the fan is just moving hot air, the SET (standard effective temperature) benefit is reduced versus moving properly mixed, cooler air.
- **No India-specific quantified numbers found** for occupant-height velocity drop, coverage-area change, dB(A), or power specifically attributable to the stratified-room case in Indian ceiling-fan literature — flagged **[UNVERIFIED]**. Most quantitative data above comes from US/EU warehouse and HVLS-fan studies, not residential 1200 mm fans in Indian rooms; applicability to a 1200 mm BLDC fan in a 2.6–3.2 m Indian room ceiling is an extrapolation, not a direct citation.

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- **Variable-speed / PI-controlled destratification is patented and field-proven at the large-space scale:** US8900041B2 ("Automatic control system for ceiling fan based on temperature differentials") describes a controller that measures ceiling and floor temperature, computes ΔT, and scales fan speed proportionally — larger ΔT → faster speed, approaching a target ΔT of zero (fully destratified) via a PI control loop ([Google Patents US8900041B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8900041B2/en)).
- **Forward vs. reverse rotation:** Conventional guidance says run fans in "reverse" (updraft) at low speed in winter to destratify without a direct draft, and in "forward" (downdraft) in summer for direct cooling. The Harvard DASH study referenced above challenges this for *residential* fans — it found continuous forward-direction, variable-speed operation can destratify a space using *less* energy than reverse-direction operation, and reverse mode can be noisier and draftier along room perimeters ([Harvard DASH thesis](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-ec98-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); [Hearth.com forum discussion of the "reversing ceiling fans in winter is dumb" claim](https://www.hearth.com/talk/threads/article-claims-reversing-ceiling-fans-in-winter-is-dumb.20832/) — this forum thread is **not a primary source** and is cited only to show the debate exists, not as evidence).
- **No optimum-speed-band number specific to residential stratified rooms was found** (e.g., "run at X RPM for ΔT of Y °C") — the warehouse-scale patent and papers describe the *control logic* (proportional/PI on ΔT) but not a universal RPM setpoint applicable to a 1200 mm Indian ceiling fan. **[UNVERIFIED]** for residential-specific numbers.
- **Resonance/beat-frequency/flicker:** No literature specific to stratification interacting with blade-pass-frequency noise was found in this pass; this is more relevant to Condition 28 (vibration/wobble) and Condition 06 (light-flicker) briefs than to the thermal case.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Ranked by expected relevance to destratification performance (reasoned from the jet-momentum/Richardson-number physics above; **no stratification-specific ranking study for small residential fans was found**, so this ranking is an engineering inference, not a cited result):

1. **Thrust per watt (sweep diameter × pitch × RPM, i.e., downwash momentum flux):** Governs how far the jet penetrates against the buoyant counter-flow (Ri depends on jet velocity²). A higher-momentum jet resists re-stratification better.
2. **Blade-to-ceiling clearance / inflow geometry:** Determines how effectively the fan can draw down the hottest layer right under the ceiling rather than re-ingesting already-mixed air; tighter clearance to the hot layer increases the fraction of truly hot air entrained each revolution. Overlaps Condition 03 (high ceilings) physics.
3. **Downrod length:** In a high/stratified-hot-roof room, a longer downrod moves the blade disk down out of the hottest boundary layer, trading some inflow-starvation benefit against jet throw loss — a genuine design tension, consistent with the known decay/throw problem raised in Condition 03.
4. **Number of blades / pitch angle:** Affects torque and achievable low-speed thrust, relevant to whether a fan can sustain a slow, continuous-mixing destratification mode economically (per the forward-low-speed-continuous strategy above) without excessive power draw.
5. **Winglets / tip shape:** Reduces tip-vortex losses, marginally improving thrust-per-watt; a second-order lever for this condition compared with 1–3.

No manufacturer currently publishes geometry recommendations specifically keyed to "ceiling-to-floor ΔT" — this ranking should be validated experimentally (Section 10) before being treated as settled.

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **Gap 1 — No residential Indian ceiling fan appears to implement literal dual-sensor (ceiling + floor) ΔT-based destratification control**, the one mechanism with the strongest industrial evidence base (warehouse studies, Section 7) and an existing patent family (Section 8) covering the most direct version of it. Opportunity: a *differently sensed* version (e.g., single ceiling-mounted ambient sensor + algorithmic stratification estimate from power draw/airflow feedback, or IMU-based proxy) could capture most of the comfort benefit while designing around US8900041B2's literal dual-sensor claim — **novelty vs. patents: needs full claim-language verification before relying on this design-around.**
- **Gap 2 — The "reverse mode helps in winter" assumption is contested even in residential-scale literature** (Harvard DASH thesis). Opportunity: a concept using continuous low-speed *forward* operation with adaptive speed (rather than marketing a reverse/winter mode) may be both more energy-efficient and avoids reverse-mode patent/claim territory tied to "winter mode" — **expected benefit: plausible energy savings based on the cited thesis, magnitude not independently quantified for a 1200 mm Indian fan [UNVERIFIED].**
- **Gap 3 — No Indian-climate-specific quantified data exists (that this search found) on how much a 1200 mm BLDC fan actually destratifies a 2.6–3.2 m Indian room under a hot RCC/tin roof**, versus the US/EU warehouse-scale evidence used throughout this report. This is the single biggest evidence gap for an India-focused team: **all physics above is extrapolated from large-space/commercial studies, not validated at residential Indian scale.**
- **Gap 4 — Hot-roof radiant contribution is conflated with air stratification** in casual treatment (and in this condition's overlap with Condition 15); a fan cannot "destratify away" radiant heat transfer from a 60–70 °C ceiling slab — only air-temperature mixing. A concept that pairs a stratification-aware fan mode with user guidance ("fan helps mix air, does not fix radiant heat — pair with roof insulation") may be a messaging/positioning opportunity rather than a pure aerodynamic one.
- **Concept directions (for an aerodynamics-led team):**
  1. **Algorithmic (single-sensor) stratification estimator + adaptive speed ramp:** Infer vertical ΔT trend from a ceiling-mounted ambient sensor's time history plus motor current/torque signature, avoiding a literal second floor sensor. Risk: control-logic patents may still be broad; needs claim review. Cost impact: low (software + existing sensor).
  2. **Continuous low-speed "mixing" mode distinct from both named "summer" and "winter" modes:** Marketed as always-on gentle circulation rather than seasonal reversal, informed by the Harvard thesis finding. Risk: differentiation is mostly marketing/control-logic, not blade aerodynamics — modest novelty. Cost impact: low.
  3. **Higher thrust-per-watt blade geometry for better jet penetration against buoyant counter-flow**, informed by the Richardson-number relationship (Ri ∝ 1/V²): incremental pitch/airfoil optimisation specifically validated against a stratified-room CFD case (see Section 10) rather than a neutral-room case. Risk: requires new CFD/wind-tunnel validation; cost impact: moderate (tooling for new blade profile).
  4. **[Speculative, needs validation] Downrod-position-adaptive mounting** that lets installers (or a future adjustable mechanism) position the blade disk to balance inflow-starvation vs. jet-throw trade-offs in very hot-roof rooms — directly follows from the Section 4 tension but **no existing evidence this has been tried**; higher risk, higher potential novelty.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Instruments:** vertical thermocouple/temperature-logger string from floor to ceiling (e.g., 5 points at 0.1 m, 0.6 m, 1.1 m, 1.8 m, ceiling) to directly measure the stratification gradient before/during/after fan operation — this is the most important and currently-missing Indian-residential data point; vane or hot-wire anemometer grid at occupant height (0.3–0.6 m lying, 1.1 m seated) to measure delivered air speed and whether it correlates with hot or mixed air; a sound level meter for dB(A) at 1 m; a power meter (watt-hour) to compute actual service value under stratified vs. unstratified test rooms; smoke/fog visualisation to observe downwash penetration depth and re-stratification/roll-back.
- **Test protocol:** Instrument a real top-floor Indian room (RCC slab or tin roof) at midday in summer, log baseline stratification with fan off, then run the fan at several fixed speeds and (if available) a variable-speed/ΔT-proportional mode, logging the floor-to-ceiling gradient and occupant-height temperature/velocity over time to see whether and how fast the gradient flattens (replicate the methodology implicit in the warehouse field studies in Section 7, scaled to a ~15–25 m² residential room).
- **Metrics to report:** vertical ΔT (floor to ceiling) over time; occupant-height air speed and temperature; time-to-steady-state; power draw and resulting service value (m³/min/W) under the stratified-room condition specifically (compare against the BEE-rated service value measured under standard IS 374:2019 lab conditions, which do **not** include a stratified thermal boundary condition — see [Bijlibachao — New BEE Star Rating Standards](https://www.bijlibachao.com/fans/new-beee-star-rating-standards-ceiling-fans.html) and [GKWinding — Ceiling Fan Star Rating Guide](https://gkwinding.com/blogs/ceiling-fan-star-rating-guide/) for the standard test basis).
- **CFD validation:** Model the room as a buoyancy-coupled (Boussinesq) rotating-fan simulation using the MRF approach validated in the warehouse literature (Section 7), with the ceiling boundary condition set to the measured hot-roof surface temperature (60–70 °C per Section 1) rather than an adiabatic/neutral wall — compare predicted occupant-height temperature and velocity against the physical measurements above. This directly follows the validated large-space methodology but must be re-validated at residential scale and with a 1200 mm BLDC fan geometry, which has not been done in the literature found.

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Existing implementation (large-space, patented):** Dual ceiling/floor temperature sensors feeding a PI controller that targets ΔT = 0, per US8900041B2 (Section 8) — proven at commercial/industrial scale (Big Ass Fans / Panasonic Atmosphere product lines, Section 6).
- **Residential smart-fan precedent:** Atomberg and other BLDC smart fans already carry ambient temperature/occupancy sensing and Wi-Fi/app connectivity for other features (sleep mode, voice control) — the sensing hardware baseline for a stratification-aware mode may already exist in current smart-fan BOMs, but **no evidence found that current products close the loop on a measured floor-ceiling ΔT** ([Atomberg smart fans](https://atomberg.com/atomberg-ceiling-fans/smart-fans)).
- **Opportunity:** IMU-based wobble/current-draw sensing (already used for balance/safety in some smart fans) could potentially be repurposed as an indirect proxy for air-density/load changes correlated with stratification, avoiding a second physical temperature sensor — **this is a speculative concept, not an existing verified product feature.**

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary:** Thermal stratification — hot air pooled under a ceiling, amplified in Indian top-floor rooms by RCC slabs and tin/asbestos roofs that can reach 60–70 °C surface temperature — is a well-studied problem at commercial/industrial scale (warehouses, hangars), where buoyancy-vs-momentum (Richardson-number) analysis and MRF/CFD modelling have established that properly driven ceiling fans can roughly halve the vertical temperature gradient (≈0.7→0.3 °C/m) and deliver double-digit percentage energy savings. The most important nuance for a residential 1200 mm Indian fan is that simply running the fan does not guarantee destratification — it can instead just recirculate hot ceiling air onto occupants — and the common "reverse the fan in winter" advice is contested even by dedicated residential-scale research. No evidence was found that any current Indian or global mass-market *residential* ceiling fan implements a genuine ceiling-floor ΔT-sensing destratification control loop (that mechanism exists only in patented industrial HVLS-fan systems), which is both the biggest unsolved gap and the area of highest existing patent risk for a literal dual-sensor approach.

**Top 5 insights:**
1. Fan-driven mixing can roughly halve a room's vertical temperature gradient in well-studied large-space cases (0.7→0.3 °C/m), but this is not yet validated at Indian-residential scale with a 1200 mm fan.
2. The "run in reverse in winter" convention is challenged by residential-scale research (Harvard DASH thesis) favoring continuous variable-speed forward operation.
3. A patented, commercially proven dual-sensor (ceiling + floor) ΔT-proportional control loop exists (US8900041B2 and family) and represents the highest patent-overlap risk for any literal implementation of this idea.
4. Indian hot-roof surface temperatures (60–70 °C) make this condition physically severe, but radiant heat transfer from the roof is a separate problem a fan cannot solve by air mixing alone — a messaging/positioning nuance as much as an engineering one.
5. No residential-scale CFD or field study specific to Indian ceiling fans and Indian room geometry/roof types was found — this is the clearest "do our own primary research" opportunity (Section 10) and the most defensible basis for a novel concept.

**Open questions / what could not be found:**
- No India-specific (IIT/IISc/CEPT) published study on residential ceiling-fan destratification.
- No verified assignee/priority-date/legal-status data for the patents listed in Section 8 (needs direct Espacenet/WIPO/InPASS/USPTO record lookup).
- No confirmed detail on whether any current Atomberg smart-fan model actually senses a ceiling-floor ΔT versus a single ambient reading.
- No quantified India-specific numbers for occupant-height velocity, coverage, noise, or power change attributable specifically to the stratified-room condition for a standard 1200 mm fan.
