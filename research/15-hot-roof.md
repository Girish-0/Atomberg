# Condition 15 — Hot-Roof Top-Floor Rooms / Tin & Asbestos-Sheet Roofs

*AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier — radiant heat from ceiling slab/sheet roofs (surface 40–55 °C in Indian summers)*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Geometry/parameters**: Top-floor room directly under an uninsulated RCC slab or a corrugated GI ("tin")/asbestos-cement (AC) sheet roof, no attic/insulation buffer. Roof *surface* temperatures commonly reach 40–55 °C under direct summer sun; measured near-surface/indoor ambient air in roofed test cells has been reported around 34.6 °C (uncoated tin), 35.8 °C (coated/standard tin) and 36.8 °C (AC sheet) versus ambient [UNVERIFIED — single comparative study, exact source/methodology not independently confirmed] ([ScienceDirect/related AC-sheet comparison](https://www.scirp.org/html/7-7700687_18472.htm)). A ceiling fan hangs in this radiant field, typically 150–300 mm below the slab/roof.
- **Prevalence in India**: Roofs are reported to contribute 50–60% of total heat gain on an unconditioned top floor ([ResearchGate — cool roof impact review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309722622_The_Effect_of_Cool_Roof_on_Built_Environment_in_Composite_and_Hot_and_Dry_Climates_of_India)). Top-floor flats, independent-house top storeys, and tin/AC-sheet-roofed rural and semi-urban housing (very common across Tier-2/3 India and the construction/labour-housing segment) are the dominant occurrence; AC sheets remain widespread in low-cost rural/industrial roofing despite health regulation on manufacturing exposure ([ScienceDirect — AC sheet manufacturing exposure](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1309104215305535)). No single national census dataset on "% of homes with hot-roof top floor" was found — housing-stock share is [UNVERIFIED].

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- A heated roof/slab creates a **buoyant boundary layer of hot, low-density air** clinging to the ceiling. The fan's blades sit inside or just below this layer, so the **inflow to the top (suction) surface of the blade is drawn from pre-heated air**, not from the cooler room volume above — effectively the fan recirculates heat rather than displacing it.
- **Buoyancy vs momentum**: whether the fan's downward jet can break through the hot layer and actually circulate is governed by the **Richardson/Archimedes number** Ri = g·Δρ·L /(ρ·U²) (buoyancy force vs fan-jet momentum force). At low fan RPM (low U), buoyant restratification can re-establish a hot layer near the ceiling faster than the jet mixes it; at high RPM the momentum-dominated jet entrains and mixes the hot layer into the room, which **destratifies** air temperature but does not change the *radiant* exchange between the hot roof and the occupant below — only the sensible/convective exchange.
- The occupant experiences two independent heat-exchange channels this condition stresses:
  1. **Convective/comfort channel**: elevated air speed at the body increases convective and evaporative heat loss (the standard "feels cooler" effect modeled by ASHRAE 55's elevated-air-speed PMV-SET correction).
  2. **Radiant channel**: a hot ceiling radiates directly onto the occupant's top surfaces regardless of air speed — a ceiling fan **does not reduce mean radiant temperature (MRT)**; it only raises convective heat loss to offset it. ISO 7730 treats this as **radiant temperature asymmetry**: for a warm ceiling, discomfort complaints become significant above roughly 5 K asymmetry (Category A/B) to 7 K (Category C) ([ScienceDirect — ceiling heating allowable surface temp](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352710224025877); general ISO 7730 asymmetry limits, [ISO 7730:2005 PDF](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/39155/9632a0563ca742209edb45856ff69296/ISO-7730-2005.pdf)). With roof surfaces at 40–55 °C against a ~27–30 °C room, asymmetry can be far beyond these comfort limits, meaning **no realistic ceiling-fan air speed fully compensates** — a physics limit the product team should accept rather than try to engineer around with blades alone. [UNVERIFIED — direct asymmetry numbers for 40–55 °C Indian roofs specifically; the 5–7 K thresholds are generic ISO 7730 figures, not India-specific validation.]
  3. The blade's own underside also absorbs re-radiated/convected heat from the hot slab, slightly raising **blade surface temperature** and, for plastic blades, reducing stiffness margin (see §5).
- **Governing phenomena to cite in the CFD/physics section**: buoyancy-driven stratification, jet momentum vs buoyant restratification (Richardson number), mixed-convection boundary layer at the roof underside, radiant asymmetry (not an airflow phenomenon but the dominant comfort driver here), and ordinary tip-vortex/downwash-jet-spread physics common to all centred-fan cases.
- **Quantified literature** found: a CBE-Berkeley study on *radiant floor cooling systems* (not roof heating, but same coupling physics) found that downward-blowing ceiling fans increased radiant slab cooling capacity by 16% at 24 °C and 26% at 26 °C room temperature via enhanced convective heat transfer ([CBE Fans Guidebook](https://cbe-berkeley.gitbook.io/fans-guidebook/full-guidebook/conventional-hvac-vs.-ceiling-fans-integrated-hvac)) — directionally relevant evidence that fan-driven convection materially changes the *convective* share of a radiant exchange, even though this specific study is about a cold slab, not a hot roof. No Indian or international study was found that directly quantifies % comfort improvement or air-speed need specifically for ceiling-fan-under-hot-tin-roof; this remains a **gap** (see §9). [UNVERIFIED for direct hot-roof quantification.]

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Because the dominant discomfort mechanism here is **radiant**, not just still-air heat buildup, the practical lever is to run the fan at or near **maximum rated RPM** to maximize convective/evaporative offset (ASHRAE 55 allows average elevated air speed up to 0.8 m/s without occupant control, higher with local control) ([CBE Thermal Comfort Tool](https://cbe-berkeley.gitbook.io/thermal-comfort-tool/documentation/fans-heatwaves); [ASHRAE 55 addendum](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/55_2017_d_20200731.pdf)).
- No resonance/beat-frequency literature specific to hot-roof operation was found; the thermal condition itself does not change blade-pass-frequency acoustics — that is governed by blade count/RPM independent of roof temperature. [UNVERIFIED — no source found tying roof heat to acoustic resonance.]
- **Reverse/updraft mode** is not useful here: reverse mode is meant to gently push warm ceiling-layer air outward/upward for winter destratification at low speed; under direct hot-roof radiant load in summer, forward high-speed downdraft (maximum convective cooling at occupant level) is what the physics and ASHRAE guidance support, not reverse mode. [Reasoned inference, not directly sourced for this specific roof case — mark UNVERIFIED as an extrapolation.]
- No published "optimum speed band" specific to hot-roof top floors was located; BLDC variable-speed control simply lets the user/the fan's own sensing push to the top of the comfort-offset curve.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked for this condition)

1. **Blade-to-ceiling clearance / downrod length** — larger clearance pulls the blade's inflow further from the hottest boundary layer and gives the downwash more room to develop before reaching occupant height; manufacturers' hugger/flush designs (minimal clearance) are the worst case for this condition specifically.
2. **Sweep diameter and air delivery per watt (service value)** — larger coverage area and higher CMM both directly increase convective offset; BEE service-value star bands (3.2–4.0 m³/min/W) are a direct lever, independent of the roof issue but especially valuable where cooling is purely fan-driven ([BEE service value bands](https://indianfanassociation.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/BIS-IS-374-2019-and-Ammendment-1.pdf)).
3. **Blade pitch/chord/airfoil for high thrust at moderate RPM** — matters for sustaining high CMM without excessive noise/power, useful since occupants will likely run the fan continuously at high speed in this condition.
4. **Tip shape/winglets** — secondary; affects efficiency and noise, not the radiant problem itself.
5. **Motor-housing/canopy shape and hub size** — low priority for this condition; mainly affects blockage of downwash core, not the radiant channel.
6. **Material and surface finish of the top (suction) blade surface** — a lever against *heat absorption and degradation*, not airflow (covered in §5).

No manufacturer guidance was found that explicitly recommends a *minimum* clearance for hot-roof conditions specifically (clearance guidance is generally safety/ceiling-height driven, IS 374/IEC, not thermal). [UNVERIFIED gap.]

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

- Fan blades are typically **ABS** (heat tolerance to ~80 °C) or **PP** (continuous service ~80–100 °C depending on formulation, melts near 160 °C); both show **reduced flexural modulus at elevated temperature**, raising flutter/sag risk when the blade's top surface sits close to a 40–55 °C roof for sustained periods ([Alibaba materials overview](https://www.alibaba.com/product-insights/fan-plastic-materials-blades.html); [Alibaba PP overview](https://www.alibaba.com/product-insights/fans-pp.html)). [Note: these are supplier/marketing-adjacent sources, not peer-reviewed — treat numbers as indicative, not certified.]
- Aluminium/sheet-steel blades have no comparable deformation risk at these temperatures but add weight (affecting balance and bearing load) and can corrode faster in combined heat+humidity conditions (cross-reference Condition 16).
- Motor winding insulation class dictates how much margin the motor has against ambient heat soak from the roof: Class B (80 °C rise over ambient), Class F (100 °C), Class H (125 °C) — a fan motor already running warm from a hot attic/roof space benefits from Class F/H insulation margin over Class B ([Plant Engineering — motor temperature classes](https://www.plantengineering.com/when-it-comes-to-motors-how-hot-is-hot/)). BLDC motors generally run cooler than induction motors for the same output, which is a relevant (if indirect) mitigation already used by Atomberg/Superfan-style products.
- Manufacturing implication: no condition-specific tooling change is needed, but material/colour choice (lighter blade colour, lower solar/radiant absorptivity on the top surface) could reduce blade heat soak — **no source found testing this for ceiling fans specifically**; it is an inference from cool-roof reflectivity literature, not validated on fan blades. [UNVERIFIED/extrapolated.]

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No current product addresses the radiant channel at all.** Every mainstream fan (Atomberg, Crompton, Bajaj, Orient, etc. — see §6) only offers faster air movement, which raises *convective* offset but cannot touch *mean radiant temperature*. Physics (§2, ISO 7730 asymmetry limits) suggests a 40–55 °C roof against a ~28–30 °C room can exceed comfort-asymmetry thresholds that no air speed alone fixes — a hard ceiling (pun noted) on how much a blade-only solution can achieve. This is the single biggest insight for the team: **manage expectations that "fix hot-roof discomfort via better blades alone" has a physics ceiling**; the biggest gains will combine fan control with roof-side or sensing-side innovation.
- **No commercial fan senses roof/ceiling surface temperature directly (IR) and auto-boosts speed or triggers a "hot-roof mode."** The closest patent prior art (temperature-differential control family, §8) uses an air-temperature differential, not a direct radiant/IR read of the ceiling — leaves room for a genuinely different sensing approach.
- **Blade/material heat-soak degradation over years of hot-roof exposure is unquantified** — no field-ageing data found for fan blades specifically mounted close to tin/AC roofs.
- **Concept directions** (illustrative, feasibility/novelty not fully vetted):
  1. **IR/thermopile ceiling-surface sensor + auto speed boost**: detect roof surface heat directly and pre-emptively run at max CMM before room air even heats up. Expected benefit: earlier comfort response than today's ambient-air-temperature-triggered logic. Risk: sensor cost, false triggers from direct sun through skylights. Cost: modest BOM addition (~₹50–150 per unit, [UNVERIFIED cost estimate]). Novelty: differs from differential-air-temperature patent family (§8) but needs full claim-overlap check.
  2. **Reflective/low-absorptivity top-surface coating or finish on the blade** to reduce blade heat-soak and indirectly reduce re-radiation toward occupants. Expected benefit: [UNVERIFIED — no ceiling-fan-specific test found; borrowed from cool-roof reflectivity logic, 1.5–3.9 °C indoor reductions seen in cool-roof studies, not blade studies]. Risk: coating durability, cost, aesthetics. Low novelty risk (coatings are common, low patent crowding expected but unverified).
  3. **Higher-than-standard max RPM/CMM tier specifically marketed for "hot-roof/top-floor" homes**, paired with BLDC efficiency to keep power reasonable at sustained max speed. Expected benefit: maximizes the only lever physics actually gives (convective offset). Risk: noise at max RPM, motor heat with ambient heat soak (Class F/H insulation margin needed, §5). Cost: moderate (stronger motor/bearing spec).
  4. **Bundling with passive roof mitigation guidance** (e.g., app/box-literature tip to apply roof insulation or light-colour coating) — not a fan innovation, but closes the gap the fan physically cannot close. Low cost, low novelty (marketing/education only), but addresses the real root cause.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Instruments**: IR thermometer or thermocouple array on roof underside (map 5×5 grid) to log actual roof surface temperature through the day; hot-wire or vane anemometer grid at seated (1.1 m) and lying (0.3–0.6 m) heights under the fan; globe thermometer for mean radiant temperature; sound meter (dB(A)) at 1 m; power meter on the fan circuit.
- **Metrics**: roof surface temp vs time of day, air speed profile at occupant height across fan speed settings, MRT at occupant location, calculated PMV/PPD (ISO 7730/ASHRAE 55) with and without fan running, power draw per CMM delivered, dB(A) at each speed.
- **Cheap real-room test**: Instrument an actual top-floor tin/AC-roofed room during peak summer afternoon (2–5 pm) and a control room with insulated/RCC roof; compare required fan speed to reach equivalent PMV.
- **CFD validation targets**: compare simulated air-speed field at occupant height and simulated wall/roof heat flux against the above field measurements; use the heated-roof boundary condition (measured, not assumed) as CFD input.

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Feasible and directly relevant here**: an IR/thermopile sensor pointed at the ceiling/roof could let the fan detect "hot roof" conditions distinct from generic room warmth, and auto-select max speed or a dedicated mode. No commercial implementation of this exact feature was found (§6, §8) — existing smart-control patents use room air-temperature differentials, not direct radiant sensing, suggesting this is a genuinely open feature space, though full patent-landscape clearance (§8) is incomplete.
- Existing, more common smart features (occupancy PIR, current-draw-based wobble detection, humidity sensing) are not specific to this condition and are documented in other condition briefs (05, 13, 28).

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Hot-roof top-floor rooms create a radiant heat load (roof surface 40–55 °C) that a ceiling fan cannot remove — the fan only increases convective/evaporative heat loss from the occupant, not the mean radiant temperature the hot roof delivers. Literature (ISO 7730 radiant-asymmetry limits, CBE Berkeley fan-comfort guidance) supports running fans at maximum rated air speed as the correct response, but no source found quantifies the specific comfort gain for Indian tin/AC-roofed rooms, and no mainstream fan brand markets a feature that senses roof radiant heat directly. The clearest, physics-honest opportunity is a radiant/IR-sensing auto-boost feature combined with maximizing air delivery per watt (BLDC), while being transparent that fan design alone has a comfort ceiling this condition imposes.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. The dominant discomfort mechanism is **radiant**, not airflow-structural — a different problem category from most other room-condition briefs in this set.
2. No current market product (Atomberg, Crompton, Bajaj, Orient, etc.) has a feature targeting this condition specifically — it's an open feature gap, not just an open patent gap.
3. The closest patent prior art uses **air-temperature differentials**, not direct radiant/IR ceiling sensing — leaving a plausible, differentiated sensing approach, pending full claim review.
4. Cool-roof literature (1.5–3.9 °C indoor reduction) suggests the biggest wins may be roof-side, not fan-side — fan teams should consider bundling guidance rather than over-promising a blade-only fix.
5. Blade material heat-soak (ABS ~80 °C tolerance, PP ~80–100 °C) is a real but under-documented long-term degradation risk for fans mounted close to hot sheet roofs.

**Open questions / not found**:
- No India-specific CFD or field study of a ceiling fan operating directly under a 40–55 °C tin/AC roof.
- No verified BEE-certified service-value figures could be pulled for the specific SKUs cited in §6 — the numbers given are marketing/retail-listing figures, not confirmed against the BEE database.
- Full 10–20-family patent landscape not completed this pass — only 5–6 families found via two queries; dedicated InPASS/Espacenet/WIPO search recommended before any design-around decision.
- No source quantifying % comfort or thermal-asymmetry improvement from fan operation specifically against a hot tin/AC roof (as opposed to generic elevated-air-speed comfort models).
