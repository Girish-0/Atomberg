# Condition 17 — Ceiling Fan Used Together With an Air Conditioner

*AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier. Prepared for Atomberg hackathon team, aerodynamics focus. Marketing claims are separated from tested data throughout; items without a verifiable primary source are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Setup**: A split or window AC discharges a cold, high-momentum horizontal (or near-horizontal) jet into the room — typically 8–15°C supply air at the sidewall/high-wall unit outlet — while a ceiling fan runs simultaneously at low-to-medium downward speed to circulate the cooled air and raise perceived comfort at a higher thermostat setpoint. This is the dominant real-world configuration being researched as "ceiling-fan-integrated air conditioning" (CFIAC) in the academic literature, and is also the single most common Indian urban-bedroom/living-room pairing today (fan + split AC).
- **How common in India**: [UNVERIFIED — no single national dataset found] Split-AC penetration in urban Indian households has grown sharply through the 2020s, and nearly every AC-equipped room in India retains the pre-existing ceiling fan rather than removing it, so fan+AC co-operation (even if unintentional/uncoordinated) is extremely common in urban apartments and commercial/office spaces; it is rare in rural/un-electrified-AC contexts where fans operate alone. No BEE/ISI survey quantifying "rooms with both fan and AC" was found in this search; this prevalence claim should be treated as directional, not a cited statistic.
- **Key geometry/parameters**: AC supply-jet throw angle and velocity (typically 3–7 m/s at the outlet, decaying with throw distance), supply air temperature (8–15°C off-coil), AC unit mounting height (1.8–2.4 m wall-mount, or ceiling-level ducted outlet for cassette ACs), ceiling fan height/sweep (per Part A context: 1200 mm sweep, 2.6–3.2 m ceiling), horizontal and vertical offset between AC outlet and fan downwash column, and relative air volumes (fan moves far more air, ~150–250 m³/min, than the AC's circulated volume, ~10–20 m³/min for a 1.5-ton split unit).

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

**Governing interaction — jet-in-crossflow.** The AC's cold horizontal supply jet acts as a crossflow relative to the fan's vertical downwash column (or the fan's downwash acts as a crossflow deflecting the AC jet, depending on relative momentum). This is the same family of phenomena as jet-in-crossflow (JICF) studies elsewhere in engineering: the fan-driven recirculation dominates where fan momentum ≫ AC jet momentum close to the fan, producing horseshoe vortices, counter-rotating vortex pairs and wake structures at the jet–crossflow interface ([mine-ventilation JICF study, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10997619/) — general JICF mechanism analogue; [UNVERIFIED] as a direct ceiling-fan citation but physically applicable).

**What the dedicated CFIAC literature found** (sidewall-supply jet + ceiling fan, UC Berkeley CBE chamber study, 5.5 × 5.5 × 2.53 m chamber, 1.5 m fan, 10°C supply air at 0.056 m³/s):
- Airflow patterns in the room are **dominated by the ceiling-fan flow field across a wide range of supply flow rates, temperatures and fan speeds** — i.e., the fan's momentum overwhelms the AC jet almost everywhere except very close to the AC outlet.
- The ceiling fan **eliminates the discrete cold supply-air jet in the occupied zone**, even when the fan is offset well to the side of the supply jet — the fan entrains and mixes the cold jet into its own recirculation loop rather than letting it travel as an intact cold stream to occupant level.
- Fan rotational speed and direction (downward vs upward) are the dominant variables controlling resulting air-speed distribution; high-speed downward operation produced the highest indoor air velocities.
([Ceiling-Fan-Integrated Air Conditioning: airflow and temperature characteristics of a sidewall-supply jet interacting with a ceiling fan, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132320300184); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338525067_Ceiling-Fan-Integrated_Air_Conditioning_airflow_and_temperature_characteristics_of_a_sidewall-supply_jet_interacting_with_a_ceiling_fan); [follow-on CFD/IAQ study, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S036013232400475X))

**Thermal comfort consequence** (same chamber, human-subject study, 23/26/28°C ambient, 4 fan modes): the fan's downwash pulls cold, denser AC-conditioned air down into the occupied zone efficiently, letting rooms run at 26–28°C with fan-on and still achieve acceptance rates comparable to 23°C fan-off reference conditions (73.7–100% acceptance across conditions; near-neutral sensation at 28°C + low downward fan vs 26°C reference). The **standard effective temperature (SET) model** — not simple PMV/PPD — is identified as the correct index for evaluating this combined condition, since PMV/PPD under-credits the localized, non-uniform elevated-air-speed cooling effect; SET-based corrective power correlated with measured thermal sensation at R² = 0.9. ([Ceiling-fan-integrated air-conditioning: thermal comfort evaluations, Buildings & Cities journal](https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.137))

**Draft-risk / cold-discomfort physics.** ASHRAE 55 caps air speed at **0.15 m/s for operative temperatures below 22.5°C** to avoid local cold draft discomfort, and allows up to **0.8 m/s** at warmer operative temperatures (uncontrolled spaces) as the elevated-air-speed offset — equivalent to about a 3 K reduction in required comfort temperature ([SimScale ASHRAE 55 summary](https://www.simscale.com/blog/ashrae-55-thermal-comfort/); [ASHRAE 55 addenda, ashrae.org](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/55_2013_b_20141209.pdf)). This matters directly for fan+AC: as the room cools toward/below ~22.5°C, the same fan speed that was comfortable at 28°C can push local air speed over the draft-risk threshold, especially at ankle height from a downward-blowing fan — a documented complaint mode. Reversing the fan to upward (updraft) in cool conditions removes the cold draft in the "dumping zone" without worsening overall cool sensation elsewhere ([UC Berkeley IEQ "Moving air for comfort", escholarship.org](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6d94f90b/qt6d94f90b.pdf)).

**Non-dimensional framing (from Part A's listed numbers, applied qualitatively — no fan+AC-specific Re/Ri values were found in the literature search, so treat the following as reasoning from first principles, [UNVERIFIED] as quantified values for this exact condition):** The relevant local momentum ratio is the fan-jet-to-AC-jet momentum flux ratio; where this ratio is large (typical residential split-AC vs 1200 mm ceiling fan), the AC jet is a passive tracer entrained into the fan's recirculation rather than an independent flow structure — consistent with the CBE chamber finding above. Where the ratio is small (e.g., a high-velocity cassette AC jet aimed directly across the fan's downwash column, or AC mounted very close to the fan), local jet deflection and shear-layer turbulence at the interface would be expected, analogous to classical jet-in-crossflow deflection correlations; no study quantifying this specific residential geometry was located.

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Fan rotational speed and direction are the two dominant levers found in the CBE studies: low-downward speed sufficed to bring 28°C to near-neutral; medium-downward raised velocities to ~1.6 m/s directly under the fan — likely exceeding comfortable draft limits at lower ambient temperatures.
- **Optimum speed band**: no single universal number is published; the literature frames it as speed-and-setpoint co-tuned via SET, not a fixed RPM. For BLDC fans (variable speed, typically 300–350 rpm max at 1200 mm sweep), the practical implication is a **lower speed requirement when paired with AC than when the fan works alone against ambient heat** — because the AC has already removed the sensible heat load; the fan's job shifts from "evaporative/convective cooling of the occupant" to "mixing/de-stratifying the AC's cold air" and avoiding cold-spot stagnation.
- **Resonance/beat-frequency/noise**: no literature was found specifically on blade-pass-frequency beating with AC compressor/fan noise; flagged as [UNVERIFIED] — plausible but unverified acoustic interaction (AC indoor-unit fan RPM and ceiling-fan blade-pass frequency could produce an audible beat in quiet bedrooms, but no measurement study was located).
- **Reverse/updraft mode**: supported by comfort literature only for the *cool-season heating* and *cold-draft mitigation* cases (destratification, reducing ankle-level cold-draft complaints), not demonstrated specifically for simultaneous AC cooling in the sources found.
- **Variable/pulsed/"natural breeze" modes**: not evaluated in any fan+AC-specific study found; Atomberg and competitors market "Boost," "Sleep," and timer modes for their BLDC fans ([Atomberg product listings](https://atomberg.com/products/atomberg-renesa-elite-bldc-motor-3-blade-ceiling-fan); [Home Assistant Atomberg integration docs](https://github.com/dasshubham762/atomberg-integration) confirm 6 speed steps + Boost + Sleep + Timer), but no published test shows these modes specifically tuned for AC co-operation — this is a marketing-feature gap, [UNVERIFIED] as tested AC-synergy performance.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked for this condition)

Because the dominant lever found in evidence is *fan-driven mixing overwhelming the AC jet*, the geometry priorities differ from a standalone-cooling fan:

1. **Downwash column uniformity / low-speed efficiency** — since the required speed band with AC assistance is lower, blade sections and pitch optimized for good air delivery at low RPM (where many stock blades stall/separate, per Part A §27 on low-Re behavior) matter more than peak high-speed air delivery.
2. **Blade count / sweep vs horizontal spreading** — wider, more uniform horizontal spread at moderate height helps intercept and mix the AC's horizontal jet into the downwash rather than letting it skim past at ceiling level toward the far wall.
3. **Hub/motor housing size** — a smaller hub blockage is less relevant here than in condition 6; priority drops.
4. **Downrod length / clearance** — matters indirectly: a fan mounted too close to the AC's outlet plane will intercept the jet at high local velocity (abrupt mixing, more noise/turbulence); one mounted far will let the cold jet fall before the fan can mix it (cold-floor pooling risk). No quantified optimum offset distance was found in literature — **flagged as an open design question**, not an established lever.
5. **Tip shape / winglets** — lowest priority for this specific condition; relevant mainly to tip-vortex noise, not to AC-jet interaction.

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **Gap 1 — No automatic fan-speed/AC-state coordination in the Indian market.** The only verified, quantified coordination result (36% compressor energy savings, [ScienceDirect field study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378778821006034)) comes from a US commercial automated-fan retrofit, not a standard residential ceiling fan. Indian BLDC fans (Atomberg, Crompton, Havells) have the IoT/IR hardware already (confirmed via product pages and Home Assistant integrations) but no confirmed SET- or AC-state-aware control logic.
- **Gap 2 — No published guidance on AC-outlet-to-fan geometric offset.** No source quantifies an optimal horizontal/vertical distance between a split-AC wall outlet and the ceiling fan's downwash column for Indian room sizes/geometries. This is squarely an aerodynamics-team opportunity (CFD + chamber test), not yet closed by anyone found in this search.
- **Gap 3 — Draft-risk crossover point undefined for Indian context.** ASHRAE 55's 0.15 m/s / 22.5°C threshold is a general standard, not validated against Indian split-AC + fan combinations at Indian humidity levels.
- **3–5 concept directions:**
  1. **AC-state-aware fan speed control** (IR/current-sensing on the AC + Atomberg's existing BLDC speed control) that automatically steps fan speed down as room temperature falls, targeting the SET-optimal point rather than a fixed user-selected speed. Expected benefit: comparable to the 10–36% compressor savings range found in literature [range cited from different studies/contexts, not a single verified number for this exact product]. Risk: requires a sensor/communication retrofit; novelty vs. patents above is moderate (US8900041B2, US11449020B2 cover adjacent ground) — needs design-around review.
  2. **Low-speed-optimized blade aerodynamics** (per §4, rung 1) since fan+AC needs less peak air delivery and more low-RPM efficiency than a standalone cooling fan. Expected benefit: [UNVERIFIED quantified number] — directionally supported by Part A's own §27 low-Reynolds concern, not by an AC-specific study.
  3. **Reverse/updraft "cold-draft mitigation" mode** specifically marketed for fan+AC bedrooms, extending the destratification/updraft comfort evidence (escholarship.org) to the AC-cooling case — currently only validated for cool-season/heating contexts in the literature, so this is an extrapolation that would need its own validation.
  4. **CFD study of split-AC-jet + ceiling-fan-downwash interaction at Indian room scale** (3×3.5 m bedroom, 1200 mm fan, typical 1.5-ton split AC) — fills Gap 2 directly; no such study was found for Indian-specific geometry.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Instruments**: hot-wire or hot-sphere anemometer grid at occupant heights (0.1, 0.6, 1.1, 1.7 m per ASHRAE comfort-measurement convention), AC supply-air thermocouple/temperature logger at the outlet and at a grid of room points, sound level meter (dB(A)) near the fan and AC, power meter on both AC compressor and fan motor, smoke/fog visualization for qualitative jet-interaction mapping.
- **Grid**: minimum a 3×3 horizontal grid at 3–4 heights, following the general approach of the CBE chamber study (which used 4 locations); denser grid directly under/near the fan and along the AC jet axis.
- **Metrics**: local air speed (m/s) and SET at each grid point; compressor runtime/duty-cycle vs fan-on/off and fan-speed; dB(A) at 1 m; power draw (W) of both devices; thermal-sensation subjective votes if human subjects are available.
- **CFD comparison**: model the AC supply jet as a cold inlet boundary condition and the fan via rotating-reference-frame (MRF) or sliding mesh (per Part A's listed approaches); validate against the above anemometry/thermocouple grid before trusting CFD-only conclusions, since no public CFD validation for this exact Indian-scale geometry was found in this search.

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Existing capability**: Atomberg and competitor BLDC fans already carry IR remote + app/IoT connectivity and multiple discrete speed steps, confirmed via product listings and third-party Home Assistant integrations ([dasshubham762/atomberg-integration](https://github.com/dasshubham762/atomberg-integration); [selvakk2k/ha-bldc-fan-ir](https://github.com/selvakk2k/ha-bldc-fan-ir)). This is existing infrastructure a coordination feature could build on without new hardware.
- **What's missing**: no confirmed product (Indian or global, consumer-grade) that senses AC on/off state or room temperature trend and auto-adjusts ceiling fan speed accordingly, apart from the commercial/industrial automated-fan systems in the 99-fan field study. This remains a genuine open opportunity rather than a solved problem.
- **Candidate sensing approaches** (not validated by any source found, offered as directions only): current-draw sensing on the AC circuit, IR snooping of AC remote commands (feasible given fans already have IR receivers), or simple room-temperature-trend-based fan speed scheduling.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: When a ceiling fan runs together with a split/window AC, published chamber research (UC Berkeley CBE) shows the fan's downwash dominates and effectively erases the AC's discrete cold jet in the occupied zone, mixing cold air down efficiently enough that rooms can run at 26–28°C with a fan and still match the comfort of 22–23°C with AC alone — provided the SET model (not simple PMV/PPD) is used to judge comfort. Real-world automated fan+AC staging has demonstrated as much as 36% compressor energy savings in a two-year commercial field study, but no equivalent automatic-coordination feature was found in any current Indian ceiling-fan product; today's Atomberg/Crompton/Havells fans have the smart hardware but apparently not the AC-aware control logic. The main unresolved risk is cold-draft discomfort as room temperature falls toward the ASHRAE 0.15 m/s/22.5°C threshold, and the main unresolved aerodynamics question is the optimal AC-outlet-to-fan geometric offset for Indian room/unit sizes — neither was quantified in any source found.

**Top 5 insights:**
1. Fan-driven mixing dominates AC-jet behavior almost everywhere in the room except right at the AC outlet ([ScienceDirect CFIAC airflow study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132320300184)).
2. SET, not PMV/PPD, is the validated comfort metric for this combined condition ([Buildings & Cities journal](https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.137)).
3. Automated fan+AC staging has a verified 36% compressor-energy-savings precedent — but only in a US commercial automated-fan context, not in any standard Indian residential fan ([ScienceDirect field study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378778821006034)).
4. Cold-draft risk (ASHRAE 0.15 m/s below 22.5°C) is a real, standard-defined constraint that caps how much fan speed helps once the AC has already cooled the room ([SimScale/ASHRAE 55 summary](https://www.simscale.com/blog/ashrae-55-thermal-comfort/)).
5. No Indian ceiling-fan brand was found with a verified, published AC-state-aware automatic speed control feature — a clear, currently-open market gap despite the hardware (IoT/IR) already existing in their product lines.

**Open questions / not found in this research pass:**
- Quantified prevalence of fan+AC co-use in Indian homes (no BEE/ISI survey located).
- Optimal AC-outlet-to-fan offset distance for Indian room geometries (no study found).
- Full patent landscape beyond Google Patents web search (Espacenet, WIPO Patentscope, InPASS not queried in this session — tool access limitation).
- Any blade-pass-frequency/AC-compressor acoustic beat-frequency interaction (no study found).
- Indian-climate/humidity-specific validation of the SET-based comfort findings (all comfort studies found were US chamber/office based).
- Full claim text, assignee, priority date and legal status for every patent listed in §8 (only search-snippet-level detail retrieved).
