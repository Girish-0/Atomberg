# Condition 19 — Cross-Ventilation (Door–Window Drafts) and Natural Wind

Evidence dossier for AtomQuest 2026. Condition: an open door and window create a horizontal through-draft of ~0.3–2 m/s across a room while a ceiling fan runs. Researched as a jet-in-crossflow problem.

## 1. Condition definition

- **Geometry**: a ceiling fan's downwash is a vertical jet (axisymmetric, actuator-disk-like) of diameter ≈ fan sweep D, discharging into a room that also carries a horizontal crossflow generated by wind-driven or buoyancy-driven cross-ventilation between an inlet opening (window) and outlet opening (door, or opposite window).
- **Typical magnitudes in Indian homes**: Cross-ventilation "comfort" air speeds are usually quoted at 0.19–0.83 m/s in CFD studies of Indian house layouts, with some wind-tunnel/field configurations reaching 1–2 m/s depending on opening size and wind incidence [UNVERIFIED — specific number ranges vary widely by source; see studiomatrx.org summary] ([Studio Matrx – Cross Ventilation in Indian Homes](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/cross-ventilation-indian-homes), [Studio Matrx – Understanding Cross Ventilation](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/understanding-cross-ventilation-india)). Outdoor prevailing wind speeds at 10 m height for major Indian cities are 2.7–3.6 m/s (Mumbai 3.6, Chennai 3.4, Kolkata 2.7, Bengaluru 2.8) [UNVERIFIED original source not retrieved directly] (cited within the same Studio Matrx search summary).
- **Design rule**: the governing opening (smaller of inlet/outlet) sets the throughput; a smaller inlet than outlet accelerates the indoor jet (per passive-ventilation literature) ([Wikipedia – Passive ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_ventilation)).
- **Prevalence in India**: Cross-ventilated plans (opposing or adjacent openings) are extremely common in independent houses and older apartments in hot-dry and composite climates (Gujarat, Rajasthan, Deccan plateau) and are a core passive-cooling strategy promoted in Indian vernacular and CSIR guidance; sealed/single-aspect apartments in dense urban high-rises reduce its occurrence [UNVERIFIED — no single national statistic on % of homes with openable cross-ventilation found]. CSIR has published ventilation guidelines for Indian residential/office buildings (post-COVID), implying cross-ventilation is treated as a standard design lever ([NITI Aayog — CSIR Ventilation Guidelines PDF](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-02/VentilationGuidelines-and-VU-C-Disinfection-Technology-for-mitigation-ofSARS-CoV-2.pdf)).

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

This is a textbook **jet-in-crossflow (JICF)** configuration: the fan's downwash is the "jet," the room's door-to-window draft is the "crossflow."

- **Governing non-dimensional parameter — velocity ratio r = U_jet / U_crossflow.** Low r → jet bends over quickly and hugs toward the floor/wall in the crossflow direction; high r → jet penetrates deep (closer to an undisturbed vertical column) before bending ([The jet in crossflow, Physics of Fluids review](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/pof/article/26/10/101303/103784/The-jet-in-crossflow); [ResearchGate — structure of jet in crossflow at low velocity ratios](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241371515_The_structure_of_a_jet_in_cross_flow_at_low_velocity_ratios)). For a typical fan, downwash core velocity near the blades is on the order of 1–2 m/s (see §3), i.e. the same order of magnitude as a 0.3–2 m/s cross-draft — so r is frequently close to 1, meaning the two flows are evenly matched and strong deflection/asymmetry should be expected rather than a negligible perturbation.
- **Jet deflection behaviour (JICF literature, generalised to this geometry)**: the jet trajectory passes through an initial near-field section with little deflection, a deflection section where it bends from vertical toward horizontal, and a far-field "run-through" section where it is essentially carried along with the crossflow direction. Deflection angle decreases (jet survives more vertically) as the velocity ratio increases ([PMC — jet ventilation in crossflow, confined mine spaces](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10997619/), [Nature Sci Reports version](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-58267-5)). Classic vortex structures reported for JICF — counter-rotating vortex pair, jet shear-layer (Kelvin-Helmholtz) vortices, horseshoe vortices, wake vortices — are described in Fric & Roshko's foundational 1994 JFM work [UNVERIFIED full equations not retrieved, summarized secondhand] ([jet-to-crossflow velocity ratio topic summary, Science.gov](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/j/jet-to-crossflow+velocity+ratio)).
- **Practical consequence for the fan**: the symmetric floor-spread "doughnut" pattern of an undisturbed fan (radially outward spread, wall-climb, return at the ceiling) is skewed downstream of the cross-draft. Coverage shifts off-centre; the upstream side of the room (toward the inlet opening) may see reduced air movement and a shorter "reach," while the downstream side gets reinforced flow. In the limit of a strong crossflow (r ≪ 1), the fan's jet is effectively swept sideways before it reaches occupant height, reducing the usable downdraft intensity directly under the fan.
- **Cooperative vs. opposing regimes**: when the fan's downwash direction and the room's natural cross-draft reinforce each other at occupant height (e.g., both moving air toward the same general exit path), perceived air movement and comfort should improve; when they oppose or shear against each other, turbulence intensity rises but net directed air motion at occupant height may actually fall. No quantified Indian-room experimental study of this specific "fan + cross-draft" interaction was found in this search pass; this is inferred by extension of JICF and natural-ventilation CFD literature — marked [UNVERIFIED — extrapolated, not a direct measurement].
- **Closest direct analogue found**: the Center for the Built Environment (CBE), Berkeley ran a chamber study with "supply air emerg[ing] from a high sidewall vent directed toward a ceiling fan" under two conditions — fan on the jet centerline, and fan offset to the side of the jet — explicitly to see how a ceiling fan interacts with an external horizontal jet. This is structurally the same crossflow-interaction problem (there, mechanical supply air; in condition 19, natural wind) ([CBE Ceiling Fan Design Guide PDF](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/CBE-Ceiling-Fan-Design-Guide-V0.pdf)). Detailed quantitative results from this chamber study were not extracted in this pass — [UNVERIFIED, recommend retrieving the full PDF for numbers].

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Raising fan RPM raises the jet's bulk velocity U_jet and thus the velocity ratio r = U_jet/U_crossflow, which should make the downwash more resistant to deflection by a cross-draft — i.e., **higher speed partially "defends" the fan's coverage pattern against an external draft**, by the same logic that governs jet penetration in JICF literature ([Physics of Fluids review](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/pof/article/26/10/101303/103784/The-jet-in-crossflow)). This is a physically reasonable inference but no fan-specific experimental confirmation under cross-ventilation was found — [UNVERIFIED].
- CBE field measurements in 4 Californian commercial buildings found ceiling fans running downward (forward mode) produced mean air speeds of 0.2–1.8 m/s depending on speed/mode, versus 0.2–0.5 m/s in upward (reverse/updraft) mode ([CBE research page](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/research/epic-integrating-smart-ceiling-fans-study/)). This gives a real-world envelope for "typical achievable fan air speed," useful for comparing against the 0.3–2 m/s cross-draft range: the two are of comparable order, reinforcing the §2 point that r ≈ 1 is a common operating regime indoors.
- A separate CFD case study of a 60 W ceiling fan found peak induced velocity of 1.53 m/s near the blades, decaying to >0.6 m/s toward the floor ([ResearchGate figure/CFD image](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-CFD-analysis-of-60-W-ceiling-fan_fig5_337633673)) [UNVERIFIED — single data point from a figure caption, methodology not reviewed].
- **Reverse/updraft mode under a cross-draft**: no literature specific to this combination was found. Reasoning by extension of §2: if the room's cross-draft already provides lateral motion, switching the fan to updraft (weaker induced speeds per the CBE data above, 0.2–0.5 m/s) may add little useful comfort and could work against the cross-draft rather than with it — [UNVERIFIED, flagged as a testable hypothesis, not a cited finding].
- No resonance, beat-frequency or blade-pass-tone issues specific to cross-ventilation were found in this search; this interaction is aerodynamic/momentum-driven, not acoustic, so such issues are not expected to be a primary concern for this condition specifically (as distinct from Conditions 05/28 on obstructions/wobble).

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Ranked by plausible impact on resisting/exploiting a cross-draft (inferred from JICF physics in §2; none of these rankings were found validated in fan-specific literature — all [UNVERIFIED] as applied to this exact condition):

1. **Thrust per watt / disk loading** — a fan geometry (blade number, chord, pitch, airfoil camber) that produces a higher jet velocity at a given power raises the effective velocity ratio r, making the downwash more resistant to crossflow deflection. This is the single largest lever by JICF scaling (deflection decreases monotonically with increasing r).
2. **Sweep diameter D** — a larger D spreads thrust over a wider jet, which in classical JICF literature penetrates differently than a narrow, high-momentum jet; the trade-off between "wide, gentle" vs. "narrow, forceful" downwash under a lateral draft is a candidate design question, not yet answered by any source found.
3. **Blade-to-ceiling clearance / downrod length** — affects the jet's "initial, undeflected" zone length before it is exposed to the room's cross-draft; a longer downrod gets the high-momentum core closer to occupant height before deflection has accumulated, but this trades off against low-ceiling constraints (Condition 02) and wobble risk on long downrods (Condition 03/28).
4. **Blade tip shape / winglets** — affects tip-vortex strength and jet coherence length, which plausibly affects how quickly the jet shear layer is disrupted by an external crossflow, but no quantified source was found.
5. **Number of blades / pitch angle** — second-order effect on swirl and jet "tightness"; no direct evidence found.

No manufacturer or academic source was found that explicitly optimises ceiling-fan blade geometry *for* a cross-ventilated room. This appears to be a **genuine literature gap** (see §9).

## 7. Academic and industry research

- **CBE Berkeley – Ceiling Fan Design Guide / Guidebook on Fans for Cooling People**: contains a chamber experiment with a high-sidewall supply-air jet directed at a ceiling fan (fan on jet centerline vs. offset), which is the closest literature analogue to the fan-vs-crossflow interaction in this condition. Method: controlled chamber, instrumented air-speed grid. Limitation: the "crossflow" there is a mechanical HVAC supply jet, not natural wind/door-window draft, and it wasn't reviewed in full detail in this pass ([PDF](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/CBE-Ceiling-Fan-Design-Guide-V0.pdf)).

### Validated CFD approach (synthesised from the above, for a team building their own model)
- Represent the fan as either a **momentum/body-force source** (faster, needs calibration against measured velocity field) or a **sliding-mesh / MRF rotating blade model** (higher fidelity, higher cost); the literature above uses measured velocity fields as fan boundary conditions when body-force models are used.
- Turbulence model: standard k-ε is reported as commonly used for ceiling-fan CFD in the literature found.
- For the crossflow: model the room with explicit inlet (window, velocity or pressure boundary condition from local wind data) and outlet (door) boundary conditions, following standard cross-ventilation CFD practice (as in the louvered-building and JAFM cross-ventilation studies cited above).
- Validate against hot-wire/vane-anemometer grid measurements at occupant height (see §10).

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No product senses horizontal/ambient air draft and adapts fan behavior.** Every brand searched (Indian and global) controls fan speed by user input or temperature/occupancy — not by detecting an open-window cross-draft. This is a clear, unaddressed gap given how common natural ventilation is in Indian homes.
- **No published aerodynamic design guidance exists for "crossflow-resistant" or "crossflow-cooperative" blade/jet geometry.** The jet-in-crossflow literature (non-fan) shows velocity ratio r is the dominant parameter, but nobody has translated this into a ceiling-fan design rule (e.g., "for rooms with typical 0.5–1 m/s cross-drafts, design thrust/disk-loading ≥ X to maintain downwash penetration").
- **Concept directions** (all [UNVERIFIED] speculative, physics-plausible, not validated by any source found):
  1. **Draft-adaptive RPM boost**: an ultrasonic or hot-wire anemometer on the fan body or downrod detects lateral air movement; firmware briefly raises RPM to restore a higher velocity ratio r and preserve downward coverage when a strong cross-draft is detected. Risk: added cost/complexity, BEE star-rating implications of higher average power draw, sensor drift/calibration in dusty environments (cf. Condition 20).
  2. **Directional/asymmetric blade pitch** (variable or fixed cyclic pitch, akin to helicopter cyclic control) to pre-bias the jet against the expected draft direction, exploiting the predictable room geometry (door/window locations are fixed per installation). Risk: mechanical complexity, cost, novelty vs. existing cyclic-pitch patents in other fan domains needs checking.
  3. **"Cooperate, don't fight" control mode**: instead of resisting the cross-draft, orient/tune the fan (where oscillating or multi-directional designs exist) to work with the natural draft's direction, amplifying natural ventilation rather than opposing it — potentially a software/mode feature rather than a blade redesign, lower cost.
  4. **Tighter jet column geometry** (cf. Hunter's AirColumn stacking concept) marketed specifically for naturally-ventilated/semi-open rooms, where a higher-momentum, narrower jet should resist deflection better than a wide, diffuse one per JICF scaling.
  5. **Hybrid ventilation advisory mode**: use the same sensor as (1) not to change fan behavior but to advise the user (app notification) on optimal window/door opening combined with current fan speed for maximum natural+mechanical cooling — a low-risk, low-cost, software-only MVP.
- **Expected benefit**: not quantified by any source found; this is the single biggest unverified gap in this report. A team should expect to generate its own CFD/experimental numbers (§10) since no existing literature quantifies "% coverage loss under a 0.3–2 m/s cross-draft" for a residential ceiling fan.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Cheap real-room test**: Use a controllable cross-draft (a desk fan or calibrated blower through one window/door as a repeatable "crossflow" surrogate, rather than relying on variable natural wind) at 2–3 target speeds spanning 0.3–2 m/s, measured with a hand-held vane anemometer at the opening.
- **Instrumentation**:
  - Vane or hot-wire anemometer grid at occupant height (1.1 m seated, 0.3–0.6 m lying) across a horizontal plane under and around the fan — minimum a 3×3 or 5×5 grid per the CBE/JAFM methodology style.
  - Smoke or fog visualisation to qualitatively capture jet deflection trajectory and confirm/calibrate against JICF trajectory expectations (§2).
  - Sound meter (dB(A)) and power meter to log any speed-compensation trade-offs if testing a draft-adaptive RPM concept (§9.1).
- **Grid points / metrics**: velocity magnitude and direction at each grid point, at 2+ fan speeds, at 2+ crossflow speeds (including zero, as baseline), repeated with fan centered vs. off-centre relative to the draft path (links to Condition 07 off-centre mounting).
- **CFD comparison**: build the room as a CFD domain with the fan as a body-force/MRF source (per §7 synthesis) and explicit window/door velocity or pressure boundary conditions; validate simulated velocity-grid output against the measured grid above before trusting CFD-only predictions of coverage loss or any proposed design lever (§4, §9).

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Feasible sensing approaches** (none found implemented commercially for this specific purpose): ultrasonic anemometer or hot-wire/hot-film sensor mounted on the fan canopy or downrod to detect ambient horizontal air speed and direction; this is standard off-the-shelf anemometer technology (widely available for weather/IoT use, e.g. consumer wind-speed sensor modules), just not yet integrated into a ceiling fan product for this purpose based on this search.
- Existing fan "smart" sensing in the market (per §6/§8 patents) is centered on temperature, occupancy, and sound — not ambient wind/draft. This reinforces §9's identification of a genuine product gap.
- IMU-based wobble sensing (relevant to Condition 28, not 19) is a related but distinct sensing modality; it would not detect a horizontal room draft.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Condition 19 (ceiling fan operating under a natural door-window cross-draft) is best understood through jet-in-crossflow (JICF) physics, where the fan's vertical downwash plays the role of the "jet" and the room's draft plays the role of the "crossflow." The key non-dimensional parameter is the velocity ratio r = U_fan / U_draft; because typical Indian cross-ventilation speeds (roughly 0.3–2 m/s, more often cited around 0.2–0.8 m/s in CFD studies) are the same order of magnitude as typical fan-induced air speeds (0.2–1.8 m/s per CBE field data), r is frequently close to 1 — meaning real homes likely experience meaningful, not negligible, jet deflection and coverage asymmetry. No brand (Indian or global) and no patent found addresses this condition with adaptive sensing or crossflow-aware aerodynamic design; the closest commercial analogue is Hunter's tighter "AirColumn" jet concept and outdoor-rated high-momentum fans from Big Ass Fans/Hunter, both incidental rather than purpose-built for this condition. This is a genuine, evidence-supported white-space opportunity for an aerodynamics-led concept, but all quantitative "benefit" estimates in §9 are speculative pending the team's own measurement/CFD campaign (§10), since no existing literature was found quantifying coverage loss or gain under this specific interaction.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. The problem is rigorously a jet-in-crossflow problem; velocity ratio r = U_fan/U_draft governs deflection, giving a clean first design variable.
2. Typical Indian cross-draft speeds (~0.2–0.8 m/s, up to ~2 m/s) and typical fan-induced speeds (~0.2–1.8 m/s) are comparable in magnitude — this is not a fringe condition, it's an everyday regime.
3. No commercial product or patent found senses or adapts to this condition — a real gap, not a crowded space.
4. The CBE Berkeley chamber study (fan vs. sidewall jet) is the closest existing experimental analogue and should be obtained in full and re-analysed before any new testing is designed.
5. Design levers (thrust/disk loading, downrod length, tighter jet column) are inferable from general JICF scaling but have never been validated specifically for ceiling fans — this is the core research task for a hackathon team to fill.

**Open questions / what could not be found**:
- No quantified % coverage loss or air-speed-at-occupant-height change under a realistic door-window draft for a residential ceiling fan.
- No full-text access obtained to Fric & Roshko (1994) or the CBE chamber study's actual numeric results in this pass — both should be fully retrieved before proceeding.
- No confirmed patent family exists purpose-built for this exact condition — but the patent search in §8 was not exhaustive (no direct Espacenet/Patentscope/InPASS queries executed), so this should be re-verified by someone with access to those databases before a design-around claim is finalized.
- No official statistic found on what fraction of Indian homes have genuine two-sided cross-ventilation versus single-aspect/sealed rooms.
