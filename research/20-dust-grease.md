# Condition 20 — Dust, Smoke, Kitchen Grease and Pollution: Aerodynamic Evidence Dossier

*AtomQuest 2026 — ceiling fan aerodynamics research. Prepared for Atomberg hackathon team.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **What it covers**: ceiling fans operating in rooms with (a) heavy ambient dust (roadside homes, rural/dry regions, construction-adjacent areas, unpaved-road frontages), (b) cooking-oil aerosol/grease from kitchens without adequate exhaust, and (c) incense/agarbatti smoke or general particulate pollution (PM2.5/PM10) common in North Indian winters.
- **Physical form of the contamination**: loose dry dust (settles as a friable layer), grease aerosol (settles as a sticky, adherent film that itself traps further dust), and fine smoke/soot particles (adhere electrostatically, especially to fast-moving blade surfaces).
- **Deposition pattern**: literature consistently reports deposition concentrated at the **blade leading edge** — termed "Leading-Edge Dust Accumulation" (LEDA) — because the leading edge has the highest local relative velocity and boundary-layer stagnation point where particles impact and stick [UNVERIFIED mechanism detail beyond what the cited paper states] ([SSRN — Balaraman et al., *Effect of Variable Thickness Leading Edge Dust Accumulation on Performance Characteristics of Ceiling Fans*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4266582)).
- **Prevalence in India**: no national survey quantifying the fraction of Indian homes affected was found in this search [UNVERIFIED]. Qualitatively, this condition is described by manufacturers as a mass-market problem — Crompton and Atomberg both market dedicated "anti-dust" ceiling-fan lines nationally (not a niche SKU), implying commercial judgement that dust-fouled blades affect a large share of the installed base ([Crompton — "Why Anti-Dust Fans Will Change Your Life"](https://www.crompton.co.in/blogs/fans-guide/why-anti-dust-fans-will-change-your-life-for-the-better); [Atomberg — Anti-Dust Ceiling Fan](https://atomberg.com/blog/post/anti-dust-ceiling-fan)). Kitchens without range-hood exhaust and homes near unpaved roads or construction sites are the highest-exposure sub-segments [UNVERIFIED — no India-specific dust-loading dataset located].

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Mechanism**: dust/grease deposits roughen the blade surface, particularly at the leading edge where the paper above measured accumulation thicknesses of **2–12 mm**. This roughness disrupts the laminar boundary layer, triggering early (premature) transition to turbulent flow and **flow separation**, which is the same mechanism studied extensively for wind-turbine leading-edge erosion ([ScienceDirect — *Analysis of leading edge erosion effects on turbulent flow over airfoils*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096014812100375X); [ResearchGate — *Realistic Leading-Edge Roughness Effects on Airfoil Performance*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269047090_Realistic_Leading-Edge_Roughness_Effects_on_Airfoil_Performance)).
- **Effect on loading**: roughness-induced early separation reduces effective lift (reduced pressure differential driving the downwash) and increases profile drag, so the blade must work harder (higher torque/current) to sweep the same volume — consistent with reports of increased power draw on dusty fans.
- **Quantified effects found in literature**:
  - Wind-turbine-airfoil roughness studies: max lift drop of **up to 30%** under roughness; for 140 µm roughness, lift-to-drag ratio fell **40%**, corresponding to ~2.3% annual energy loss in that application — not a ceiling-fan number, included only to show the general sensitivity of airfoils to leading-edge roughness ([ResearchGate table](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Impact-of-roughness-on-airfoil-performance_tbl2_271831457)).
  - Ceiling-fan-specific (SSRN paper): LEDA of 2–12 mm thickness **weakens service value**; one secondary summary of this line of work cites **~25% airflow drop, ~15% RPM loss, ~10% increase in power draw** for a dusty vs clean fan, and states LEDA can push a fan's service value below the ASHRAE-cited minimum of 4.0 m³/(W·min) down toward ~2.5 m³/(W·min) [UNVERIFIED — this specific numeric triple (25%/15%/10%) came from a secondary aggregator summarizing the SSRN paper, not confirmed by direct access to the paper itself, which returned HTTP 403 on fetch; treat as indicative, not final]. **Action for the team: obtain the full SSRN/journal PDF directly to confirm these numbers before citing them in any claim.**
  - Grease-specific (commercial exhaust fans, not ceiling fans, but same mechanism of mass/shape change on a rotating blade): grease buildup reported to **reduce airflow by 30–50%** and to shift rotor mass by **8–25 g per blade within 1,000 operating hours**, enough to violate ISO 1940-1 balance grade G6.3 — this is from commercial kitchen *exhaust* fan literature, not residential ceiling fans, and is cited here as an analogous mechanism (uneven mass deposition → imbalance → vibration → further flow loss), not a ceiling-fan-specific number ([Jilu Kitchen — *How Grease Deposition Triggers Dynamic Imbalance*](https://www.jilukitchen.com/how-grease-deposition-triggers-dynamic-imbalance-in-commercial-kitchen-fans/); [Bryan Exhaust Service — *How Grease Affects Exhaust Fan Performance*](https://bryanexhaust.com/how-grease-affects-exhaust-fan-performance/)).
- **Imbalance/wobble**: deposits are rarely symmetric across the 2–4 blades (different distance from kitchen stove, different exposure to a window draft carrying dust), so mass and drag differ blade-to-blade, producing a once-per-revolution (1P) force imbalance — wobble — compounding the pure aerodynamic loss with a vibration problem. No ceiling-fan-specific balance-shift number was found; the exhaust-fan figure above is the closest analogue [UNVERIFIED for residential ceiling fans].
- **Resuspension of floor dust**: a DOE/LBNL (OSTI) chamber study on ceiling fans and room air cleaners found that running a ceiling fan **increased removal of ultrafine and >1 µm particles** relative to no-fan/no-cleaner baseline, attributed to increased deposition onto fan blades and other surfaces, and found **little difference in resuspension by height** — i.e., in that chamber study the fan did not strongly re-loft settled floor dust ([OSTI/LBNL — *Impact of Ceiling Fan and Room Air Cleaner on Air Mixing and Fate*](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2538429)). The full PDF could not be parsed by the fetch tool used here (binary/encoded), so chamber size, fan speed, and particle-size-resolved numbers are **[UNVERIFIED — need manual PDF read]**.
- **Smoke/haze transport**: no India-specific quantified study of ceiling fans mixing indoor smoke (agarbatti, cooking) was located in this pass; the OSTI study above is the closest quantified proxy and is about general particle mixing, not combustion smoke specifically [UNVERIFIED gap].

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- No literature was found quantifying an "optimum RPM band" specifically for dust/grease-fouled blades [UNVERIFIED — likely genuine research gap].
- Qualitative inference from the roughness-aerodynamics literature: higher RPM raises local Reynolds number at the blade, which can partially delay separation on a *clean* blade, but on a roughened/fouled blade the roughness-induced transition is largely Re-independent (trip-strip-like behaviour), so the percentage loss from fouling would be expected to persist across speed settings rather than disappear at high RPM [UNVERIFIED extrapolation — not read directly from a ceiling-fan source].
- Reverse/updraft mode, pulsed "natural breeze" mode: no evidence found that these modes change fouling rate or mitigate fouling's aerodynamic penalty [UNVERIFIED gap].
- Vibration/imbalance from uneven fouling could interact with the blade-pass and structural resonance frequencies of the fan–downrod system (see condition brief 28, "Vibration, wobble and imbalance"), but no dust-specific resonance data was found here [UNVERIFIED, cross-reference only].

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Ranked by what the evidence found actually supports (not assumed):

1. **Leading-edge surface treatment / geometry** — highest-impact lever per the SSRN LEDA study, since deposition and its aerodynamic penalty concentrate at the leading edge. A rounded, smooth, or surface-treated leading edge that resists particle adhesion is the most directly evidenced lever.
2. **Surface coating chemistry** (see Section 5) — hydrophobic/oleophobic nano-coatings are commercially deployed specifically to reduce adhesion, claimed to cut dust accumulation by **~50%** (Crompton) or **60–70%** for dust-repellent nanoparticle surfaces in general materials-science literature (not fan-specific) ([US20110076478A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20110076478A1/en)).
3. **Blade material/surface energy** — low-surface-energy engineered plastics with conductive additives (carbon black, carbon fiber, graphene) reduce electrostatic attraction of dust; one Chinese patent reports **<10% blade dust coverage for graphene-loaded blades vs ~50% for standard plastic** under continuous operation ([CN104912837A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN104912837A/en)).
4. **Blade count/chord/pitch** — no direct evidence found that these change fouling rate; their relevance here is only that fewer/smaller blades mean less total area to foul and to clean [UNVERIFIED, logical inference only].
5. **Hub/motor housing shape** — not evidenced as a dust-fouling lever in this search; relevant mainly for the unrelated conditions (cove ceilings, obstructions).

No quantitative ranking (e.g., "leading-edge treatment contributes X% vs coating Y%") was found in the literature; the ranking above is qualitative, based on which levers have *any* direct ceiling-fan or closely analogous evidence versus none.

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

- **Nano-coatings**: hydrophobic (water-repelling) and oleophobic (oil/grease-repelling) coatings are the dominant commercial response. Mechanism per manufacturer claims: low surface energy and high cross-linking density prevent a continuous dust film from forming, and the smooth finish reduces friction so particles are more easily shed ([Atomberg blog](https://atomberg.com/blog/post/anti-dust-ceiling-fan); [Crompton blog](https://www.crompton.co.in/blogs/fans-guide/why-anti-dust-fans-will-change-your-life-for-the-better); [Gold Medal — *What Are Anti-Dust Fans?*](https://www.goldmedalindia.com/blog/what-are-anti-dust-fans/)). These are **marketing-stated mechanisms**, not independently verified test data — no third-party lab report quantifying the dust-reduction percentage under Indian ambient-dust conditions was found [UNVERIFIED claims vs tested data].
- **Conductive/anti-static additives**: carbon black, carbon fiber, graphene loaded into engineering plastics reduce electrostatic charge build-up on a spinning blade, which otherwise attracts airborne dust (triboelectric charging) — supported by the CN104912837A patent's comparative dust-coverage data cited above.
- **Base blade materials unaffected by this search**: aluminium sheet, ABS, ASA, PP, glass-fibre-reinforced nylon — no condition-specific (dust/grease) comparative data on which base material fouls faster was found [UNVERIFIED gap]. General expectation (not sourced here): smoother injection-moulded plastics foul less than stamped/painted aluminium with surface texture, but this is inference, not evidence.
- **Grease-specific materials**: no ceiling-fan-specific anti-grease coating literature was found separate from the general oleophobic coating claims above; commercial kitchen exhaust equipment uses dry-lubricant coatings on blades to resist grease adhesion ([US6050258 — *Exhaust fan with dry lubricant coating*](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6050258); [US5809993](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5809993)), a plausible transferable idea but **not validated for residential ceiling fans** [UNVERIFIED applicability].
- **Manufacturing/cost**: adding a nano-coating step is an added coating/cure process in the paint line; no INR cost-delta figure was found in this search [UNVERIFIED].

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No independent, third-party-verified quantification exists (that this search could find) of how much dust/grease fouling degrades a residential Indian ceiling fan's air delivery, service value, or balance over realistic months-long exposure.** All commercial "anti-dust" claims are manufacturer-stated percentages (e.g., Crompton's 50%) without a cited independent test protocol or lab report.
- **Grease-specific ceiling-fan data is essentially absent.** All grease-fouling quantification found is from *commercial kitchen exhaust fans* (different fan type, much higher grease loading), not residential ceiling fans used near but not inside a cooking exhaust path. This is a real, large gap given how common open kitchens (without range hoods) are in Indian homes.
- **No published accelerated-ageing or field-degradation curve** (e.g., % service-value loss vs months of operation in a dusty environment) was found for any ceiling fan model.
- **Concept directions** (based only on what the evidence above supports or plausibly extends):
  1. **Leading-edge-focused coating/geometry** — since LEDA concentrates at the leading edge, a locally-reinforced, smoother, or differently-coated leading-edge strip (rather than whole-blade coating) could be a cheaper, more durable mitigation. Expected benefit: directional reduction of the dominant fouling zone; quantified benefit **not available [UNVERIFIED]** — would need to be established via the team's own dust-chamber test (see Section 10).
  2. **Anti-static conductive-filler blade material** (as in CN104912837A) — reduces triboelectric dust attraction; risk of patent overlap (flagged above) means a differentiated additive/filler chemistry or ratio would be needed.
  3. **IMU/current-draw-based fouling/imbalance detection with a "clean me" alert** — leverages the same imbalance mechanism documented in the grease/exhaust-fan literature (mass-shift → vibration) to prompt user cleaning before airflow degrades significantly; ties into Section 11 below. No existing residential-ceiling-fan implementation of this specific use case was found [UNVERIFIED — may be novel].
  4. **Sacrificial/replaceable leading-edge strip** — a cheap, swappable wear strip (akin to wind-turbine leading-edge protection tape) instead of recoating or replacing the whole blade; novelty vs patents not assessed (would need a dedicated search).
  5. **Oleophobic coating specifically rated/tested for Indian cooking-oil aerosols** (mustard/sunflower/coconut oil characteristics differ from Western test oils) — a genuine, testable differentiation opportunity, since no source found tested oleophobic coatings against Indian cooking-oil aerosol composition specifically.
- Cost impact, manufacturing feasibility, and novelty-vs-patent scoring for each of the above are **not quantified here** — this section intentionally stops at directional ideas per the evidence actually gathered, not invented numbers.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Cheap real-room test**: mount a sample fan in a controllable dust environment (e.g., a sealed test box or a real kitchen/roadside room); measure air delivery (vane anemometer grid under the fan, per IS 374 test geometry) and power draw (watt meter) at t=0 (clean) and at fixed intervals (e.g., weekly) over 1–3 months of real exposure; weigh/photograph blades to quantify deposit thickness and mass per blade (to check for imbalance, replicate the grease-fouling mass-shift logic from Section 2).
- **Grease-specific test**: replicate near a stove without a hood, using a standard Indian cooking-oil frying protocol, measuring oleophobic-coated vs uncoated blades' deposit mass and airflow over weeks.
- **Instruments**: hot-wire or vane anemometer grid (per IS 374/BEE test methodology), precision scale for blade mass, sound meter (dB(A)) to check if fouling/imbalance raises noise, power meter, optionally a laser particle counter to characterise the dust/smoke environment itself (PM2.5/PM10).
- **Metrics to report**: air delivery (m³/min) vs time, service value (m³/min/W) vs time, blade mass asymmetry (g), vibration amplitude (if an IMU or accelerometer is available), dB(A) vs time.
- **CFD**: model a blade with an added leading-edge roughness element (as a simplified roughness patch or an actual measured deposit profile, if the team scans a real fouled blade) and compare predicted lift/drag/airflow loss against the real-room measurements above. Use the same turbulence-model/mesh approach as other ceiling-fan CFD work in this dossier series (not independently validated for dust-roughness cases in this search — flagged in Section 7).

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Current-draw/vibration-based fouling detection**: plausible given that fouling is documented (in the exhaust-fan analogue) to raise both power draw and vibration via mass imbalance; no existing residential-ceiling-fan product implementing this specific "detect dust fouling via current+vibration signature" feature was found in this search [UNVERIFIED — appears to be an open opportunity rather than a solved problem].
- **IMU wobble detection** generally exists in some smart-fan contexts for balance/safety (cross-reference condition 28), but its specific use for *dust-fouling* diagnosis (as opposed to generic imbalance from installation/manufacturing defects) was not found as an existing implementation.
- **Air-quality sensing** (PM2.5/PM10, smoke) to automatically adjust fan speed for better mixing/dust removal: the OSTI chamber study (Section 2/7) supports the general idea that fan operation affects particle deposition and mixing, which is a reasonable basis for a feedback-control concept, but **no commercial product automatically modulating ceiling-fan speed based on a PM sensor reading for dust-exposure mitigation was found** [UNVERIFIED — potential white space].

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Dust, grease and smoke foul ceiling-fan blades predominantly at the leading edge, where roughening disrupts the boundary layer and raises drag while cutting lift — a mechanism well documented in the general airfoil/wind-turbine-roughness literature and specifically studied for ceiling fans in one SSRN paper on "leading-edge dust accumulation" (LEDA), though that paper's full text could not be retrieved in this session and its headline numbers (~25% airflow loss, ~15% RPM loss, ~10% power increase) come only from a secondary summary and should be independently verified. Grease-specific quantification exists only for commercial kitchen exhaust fans (30–50% airflow loss, 8–25 g/blade mass shift in 1,000 hours), not residential ceiling fans, leaving a real evidence gap for the kitchen-fan use case most relevant to Indian homes. Commercially, Atomberg and Crompton both sell "anti-dust" ceiling fans using hydrophobic/oleophobic nano-coatings, with Crompton claiming a 50% reduction in dust accumulation — but these are unverified marketing claims, not independently tested data. The patent landscape located here (6 families, not independently claim-verified) is too thin to meet the brief's 10–20-family target and must be treated as a starting point only.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. Fouling-driven performance loss is a **leading-edge-dominant** phenomenon — the highest-leverage design lever is leading-edge treatment, not whole-blade redesign.
2. **No independently verified, India-specific quantitative dataset exists** linking months of real dust/grease exposure to measured air-delivery and service-value loss in residential ceiling fans — this is the single biggest evidence gap and the most valuable thing the team could generate itself (Section 10 gives a concrete, cheap test plan).
3. Grease-fouling data is borrowed entirely from commercial kitchen exhaust fans; **residential ceiling-fan-specific grease data does not appear to exist publicly** — a genuine white-space for original research/marketing claims if the team runs its own test.
4. Uneven fouling across blades plausibly causes **imbalance/vibration**, not just pure airflow loss — this links condition 20 directly to condition 28 (vibration/wobble) and suggests a combined sensing solution (current + vibration) could serve both.
5. All current market "anti-dust" solutions are **coating-based and marketing-claimed**; no mechanical self-cleaning, sacrificial leading-edge, or sensor-triggered-maintenance product was found on the market, suggesting those directions (Section 9, items 3 and 4) are relatively open.

**Open questions / what could not be found**:
- Full text and verified numbers from the SSRN LEDA paper (403 error blocked direct access).
- Readable extraction of the OSTI/LBNL chamber-study PDF (binary parsing failed; raw PDF was saved locally for manual reading).
