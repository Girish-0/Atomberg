# Condition 23 — Mosquito Nets, Canopies and Partitions: Ceiling-Fan Aerodynamics Dossier

*Prepared for Atomberg "AtomQuest 2026" — ceiling-fan aerodynamics hackathon. Evidence dossier; marketing claims are flagged separately from tested data. Items without a verifiable primary source are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.*

## 1. Condition definition

A mosquito net (bed canopy, cloth partition, or decorative screen) is draped over or around the bed/sitting area, sitting directly in the fan's downwash path or around the occupant. The net is a **porous screen**: a woven mesh with open area ("porosity," φ) typically 50–85% for standard polyester/nylon bed nets [UNVERIFIED — exact φ of commercial Indian bed nets not found in literature; insect/greenhouse screens cited below range φ ≈ 19–69%, which is the closest quantified analogue]. Key geometric parameters: mesh size (holes/inch, commonly 150–190 denier polyester with ~1,000–1,800 holes/sq inch for WHO-grade long-lasting insecticidal nets), screen thickness, weave angle, and distance from the net to the fan sweep (canopy nets are typically 0.3–1 m below the fan, enclosing the sleeping surface at 0.4–0.6 m height).

**Prevalence in India:** Mosquito nets remain "the single most widely practised" vector-control and comfort measure in tropical/sub-tropical households (per the Ghana bed-net fan literature, used as the closest quantified proxy — direct India-specific prevalence statistics were not found) [UNVERIFIED for India-specific %]. Within India, net or curtain-partition use is common in rural and semi-urban homes year-round and in urban homes seasonally (monsoon/post-monsoon mosquito season). Decorative four-poster canopy drapes and room partition curtains near beds are a secondary but related use case, common in apartments to visually separate sleeping zones. No BIS/BEE statistic quantifying net-usage rate under ceiling fans in India was located; this is flagged as a gap for primary market research (see §9/§12).

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

**Governing picture:** The ceiling fan produces a turbulent axial jet (the downwash cone) that must cross the net before reaching the occupant. The net acts as a **flat porous screen in crossflow/normal flow**, well studied in the greenhouse-ventilation and insect-screen literature, which is the best quantitative analogue available for a bed net (no India-specific CFD study of bed nets under ceiling fans was found).

- **Pressure-drop / porosity relationship:** Flow through a screen is governed by the Forchheimer-type equation
 ΔP = (ρ·e·Y/Kp)·u² + (e·μ/Kp)·u
 where ΔP is pressure drop, u is approach velocity, e is screen thickness, ρ/μ are air density/viscosity, and Kp (permeability) and Y (inertial factor) are empirical screen constants fitted from wind-tunnel tests ([Valera et al., wind-tunnel/CFD screen characterisation, MDPI Sensors 2016, DOI 10.3390/s16050690](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/16/5/690); [Pressure-drop coefficient paper, MDPI Agronomy 2023, DOI 10.3390/agronomy13020602](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy13020602)).
- **Discharge coefficient vs. porosity:** Measured discharge coefficients (Cd,φ) for insect screens ranged from 0.151–0.325 for porosities of 26–36% at an approach velocity of 0.25 m/s; loss coefficient decreases as porosity increases ([ResearchGate: Pressure drop across insect-proof screens](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275573820_Pressure_drop_across_insect-proof_screens); [ScienceDirect: Models for characterising aerodynamics of insect-proof screens](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1537511020300258)). A widely cited practical figure: for a reference approach wind speed of 1 m/s, a fine insect-proof screen reduced inflow velocity by **~75%** [ResearchGate summary of wind-tunnel greenhouse studies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235909969_Airflow_Resistance_of_Greenhouse_Ventilators_with_and_without_Insect_Screens) — this is a greenhouse-screen result, not a bed-net-specific measurement, and is offered as an order-of-magnitude analogue [UNVERIFIED for bed-net mesh specifically].
- **Direct India bed-net/ceiling-fan data point:** A published Indian study directly measuring this condition, *"Thermal Comfort Compromise due to usage of Mosquito Net in a Bedroom with a Ceiling Fan Induced Air Velocity"* ([ResearchGate abstract page](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369561105_Thermal_Comfort_Compromise_due_to_usage_of_Mosquito_Net_in_a_Bedroom_with_a_Ceiling_Fan_Induced_Air_Velocity) — full text behind a paywall/403 on WebFetch, so only the indexed abstract-level data point could be retrieved), reports air velocity inside the net dropping to **effectively 0 m/s**, compared with **0.93 m/s** measured without the net, under the same ceiling fan operation. This is the single most important quantified finding for this condition and should be obtained in full text (via institutional access or the publishing journal) before citing further numeric detail; the ~0 m/s vs 0.93 m/s figures above come from the indexed summary, not the full paper, and are marked **[UNVERIFIED — needs full-text confirmation]**.
- **Mechanism:** The net imposes a near-total velocity sink at the boundary (high loss coefficient at low approach velocities typical of ceiling-fan-induced flow, since fan-driven air speeds at occupant height, 0.3–1.5 m/s, sit in the low-Reynolds-number, viscous-dominated part of the screen's ΔP–u curve where the loss coefficient is highest). Turbulence intensity and jet coherence are also disrupted: the net breaks up the coherent downwash into a diffuse, lower-momentum flow inside the enclosed volume, and the enclosed net volume behaves like a quasi-stagnant pocket with weak internal recirculation, trapping heat and humidity from the occupant's own body (a local micro-stratification/humidity-buildup effect, analogous to the buoyancy-vs-momentum competition in Condition 13, but at bed-canopy scale).
- **Non-dimensional framing (first-order, derived by the researcher, not sourced — to be validated with CFD/experiment):** a local screen Reynolds number based on mesh pore diameter and approach velocity controls whether flow through the net is viscous (Darcy) or inertial (Forchheimer) dominated; at typical fan-induced approach speeds (0.5–2 m/s) and bed-net pore sizes (~0.4–0.6 mm), the flow is in the mixed/low-Re viscous-dominated regime, which is precisely where loss coefficients are largest — i.e., bed nets are aerodynamically at their worst-case operating point for a ceiling fan's typical air speed. This inference follows from the Forchheimer equation and screen literature above but has not itself been published for bed nets; mark **[UNVERIFIED — inference, needs direct test]**.
- **Quantified comfort effect:** With near-zero air speed inside the net, the ASHRAE 55 elevated-air-speed comfort benefit is void: ASHRAE 55 requires average air speed (Va) > 0.2 m/s before the elevated-air-speed method can even raise the allowable operative temperature, and speeds up to 0.8 m/s (no occupant control) or 1.2 m/s (with control) are used to extend the comfort zone in hot-humid climates ([ASHRAE 55 overview, SimScale](https://www.simscale.com/blog/ashrae-55-thermal-comfort/); [ASHRAE 55 Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASHRAE_55); [Elevated air speed in hot-humid climates, ScienceDirect, DOI 10.1016/j.buildenv.2023...](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378778823001834)). A net that drops in-canopy velocity toward 0 m/s effectively removes the entire fan-driven comfort benefit for the sleeping occupant, forcing reliance on lower set-point AC or discomfort — directly relevant to the Ghana solar-net-fan rationale below.

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Because the net's pressure-loss coefficient is highest at the low approach velocities typical of fan-driven flow, simply increasing RPM is a weak lever: ΔP rises with u² (inertial term) plus a linear viscous term, so a large fraction of any speed increase is absorbed by the screen rather than reaching the occupant; no bed-net-specific RPM sweep study was found to quantify this trade-off numerically — **[UNVERIFIED — needs direct test]**.
- No literature was found on resonance, beat-frequency, or blade-pass-tone interactions specific to a nearby fabric net (the net is not rigid and unlikely to resonate structurally, unlike the beam/obstruction case in Condition 5), so this sub-topic is flagged as **[no evidence found]** rather than invented.
- Reverse/updraft mode is not applicable here (the mechanism of interest is direct downwash penetration, not destratification).
- The most relevant "speed" lever found in the literature is not the ceiling fan at all but a **secondary low-power fan placed inside the net** (see §6, §9) — the Ghana "solar net fan" / "Bͻkͻͻ system" concept uses a small pedestal fan (hypothesised, no measured W rating found) run from a solar panel + deep-cycle battery, placed inside the net itself to bypass the screen entirely rather than trying to push more air through it ([Zenodo: Briët, "Solar net fan: a potential tool to enhance bednet usage in hot climates," MalariaWorld Journal, 2012](https://zenodo.org/records/10977870)). This is a hypothesis paper only — no measured comfort/temperature outcome data were reported by the author.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

No peer-reviewed or patent source was found that engineers ceiling-fan blade geometry specifically to counter net/screen pressure loss. Reasoning from the physics in §2 (not sourced; flagged as design hypothheses, to be validated):

| Lever | Expected relevance to this condition | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Higher thrust/static-pressure capability per watt (more blades, higher pitch/camber) | Partially compensates for screen ΔP by raising available dynamic pressure before the net — but screen ΔP scaling (∝u²) limits returns | Hypothesis, unverified |
| Lower RPM, higher torque, larger sweep | Larger sweep spreads the same airflow over more net area, but does not change local loss coefficient | Hypothesis, unverified |
| Direction/aim (not applicable — fans are fixed overhead) | N/A for standard ceiling fans; a secondary in-net fan (§3, §9) sidesteps the screen entirely | Supported by Ghana net-fan concept, but that is a separate appliance, not a ceiling-fan redesign |

**Ranking for this condition:** because the dominant lever found in evidence is "bypass the screen with an auxiliary low-power device inside the net" rather than "push harder from outside it," blade/geometry levers on the ceiling fan itself rank low-impact relative to a complementary small in-net airflow device or smart-control response (§11).

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

**What no current product solves well, and why:**
1. No ceiling fan — Indian or global — offers a mode, sensor response, or geometry change specifically for net/canopy/partition use (§6). Reason: likely low perceived market size relative to engineering effort, and the fix (more RPM) has diminishing returns against screen physics (§2–3).
2. The only known mitigation (a secondary low-power fan placed inside the net) is a malaria-prevention research concept from Ghana, never commercialized as a comfort product in India, and never integrated with the ceiling fan's own control system (§3, §7).
3. No quantified Indian bed-net porosity/mesh data or BIS standard check was completed, leaving the exact design target (how much ΔP a "typical" Indian bed net imposes) unknown (§5) — a near-term, low-cost lab task (hot-wire anemometer on either side of a sample net at varying fan speeds) would close this gap cheaply.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Instruments:** hot-wire or hot-sphere anemometer (for low-speed, <1 m/s resolution needed given the ~0 m/s in-net baseline reported in §2); a small grid of 4–6 probe points inside the net at lying height (0.3–0.6 m) and seated height (1.1 m) for canopy/partition variants; smoke/fog visualisation to see whether any coherent jet survives screen penetration; sound meter and power meter to check whether any speed-boost mitigation (concept #1 in §9) crosses noise/BEE-efficiency limits.
- **Test matrix:** vary (a) fan speed/RPM across the BLDC band, (b) net porosity (source 2–3 commercial net samples of different mesh density), (c) net-to-fan vertical clearance, (d) net enclosure geometry (open-sided canopy vs. fully enclosed bed tent).
- **Metrics to report:** in-net mean and peak air velocity (m/s) at each grid point, turbulence intensity, velocity ratio (in-net / no-net at same fan speed and point) as the primary KPI, dB(A), and W.
- **CFD comparison:** model the net as a Darcy–Forchheimer porous-medium boundary condition (per §2/§7 methodology) fitted from the researcher's own wind-tunnel-style bench measurement of a sample net (push a known flow through a net sample in a duct, measure ΔP vs. u to fit Kp, Y), then simulate the full room + fan + net geometry (MRF or sliding-mesh for the blades, standard k-ω SST or similar turbulence closure) and validate against the physical grid measurements above.

## 11. Sensors and smart control

- **Feasibility:** An IMU/current-draw-only approach (as used for wobble detection, Condition 28) cannot detect a net, since a net imposes no mechanical load on the fan itself — detection would require an independent sensor (ultrasonic/IR distance sensor aimed downward to detect a canopy surface, or a simple occupant-reported "bed mode" toggle via app/remote).
- **Existing implementations:** none found that combine ceiling-fan smart control with net detection. The closest analogues are (a) temperature/humidity-triggered speed control marketed generically by several BLDC fan brands for "sleep mode" (not net-specific, and no brand-level verified spec was found in this search), and (b) the Ghana solar net-fan, which is manually operated, not sensor-driven.
- **Conclusion:** this is an open feature space; a simple manual "net/canopy mode" (fixed higher minimum speed band, perhaps paired with pulsed operation per §9 concept 3) is the lowest-risk near-term implementation, with automatic detection as a later-stage upgrade.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary:** Mosquito nets, bed canopies, and partition screens act as porous-screen obstructions directly in a ceiling fan's downwash path. Porous-screen literature from the greenhouse/insect-screen domain (the best available quantitative analogue) shows that fine mesh screens impose pressure-loss coefficients that are largest exactly in the low-velocity regime typical of fan-driven airflow, and one Indian study (abstract-level only, full text not accessible in this session) reports in-net air velocity falling from ~0.93 m/s to effectively ~0 m/s with a net in place — which, per ASHRAE 55, is below the 0.2 m/s threshold needed for any elevated-air-speed comfort benefit, effectively nullifying the fan's purpose for the enclosed occupant. No mainstream Indian or global ceiling-fan brand addresses this condition; the only related precedent is a non-commercial Ghanaian malaria-prevention research concept (a small auxiliary fan placed inside the net, bypassing the screen rather than overpowering it) and a handful of Chinese utility-model patents on fan-in-net mounting hardware, none of which touch smart control or blade aerodynamics. This represents a clear, currently unaddressed market and patent white space, but one where the physics favor an auxiliary in-net airflow device or a net-aware ceiling-fan control mode over brute-force RPM increases alone.

**Top 5 insights (ranked):**
1. The core physics is a porous-screen pressure-drop problem, not a ceiling-fan aerodynamics problem per se — the screen's loss coefficient dominates at the low velocities fans typically produce (§2).
2. One Indian data point (abstract-only) quantifies near-total velocity loss inside a net (~0.93 → ~0 m/s); obtaining this paper's full text is the single highest-priority next step (§2, §7).
3. No commercial ceiling fan — Indian or global — has a net-aware feature; this is a confirmed market and patent gap (§6, §8).
4. The only precedent mitigation bypasses the screen with a small in-net auxiliary fan (Ghana research concept), rather than trying to push more air through the net from the ceiling (§3, §9).
5. A standard-method validation path exists and is cheap: bench-measure 2–3 Indian bed-net samples' ΔP-vs-velocity curve, fit a Darcy–Forchheimer porous model, then grid-measure in-net velocity across fan speeds (§10) — this alone would convert most of this report's [UNVERIFIED] items into verified, India-specific numbers.

- Actual porosity/mesh-density/ΔP values for commercial Indian mosquito nets (as opposed to greenhouse insect screens).
- Any BIS standard governing mosquito-net mesh density or aerodynamic properties.
- India-specific statistics on the percentage of households using nets/canopies under ceiling fans.
- A complete InPASS/Espacenet/WIPO Patentscope search (this session used Google Patents only) — needed before any patent-risk conclusion.
