# Ceiling Fan Aerodynamics — Condition 25: High Occupancy (Classrooms, Offices, Prayer Halls, Gatherings)

*AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier. Condition: many occupants seated/standing under ceiling fans — body-heat plumes, occupant obstruction, CO₂/air-quality load, noise requirements. All uncited or extrapolated figures are marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.*

## 1. Condition definition

High-occupancy spaces are rooms where human density is high enough that body heat generation, occupant bodies as physical obstructions, and breathing-zone air quality become comparable in magnitude to the fan's own aerodynamic effects. Typical Indian examples: naturally-ventilated school/college classrooms (30–60 students in ~55–70 m², i.e. ~0.6–1.0 person/m²), open-plan offices, prayer halls/mosques/temples (seated, often floor-seated, dense rows), wedding/community halls, railway waiting rooms.

- Each seated adult dissipates roughly 70–130 W of sensible+latent heat depending on activity level (sedentary ≈1.0–1.2 met ≈ 70 W sensible; metabolic data from ASHRAE 55/ISO 7730 tables) **[UNVERIFIED — exact split sensible/latent not independently re-derived here, standard ASHRAE value]**. In a packed classroom (0.6–1 person/m²) this is an added internal heat load of roughly 50–100 W/m², which is comparable to or larger than solar/envelope gains in many naturally ventilated Indian classrooms.
- Field studies in India report classroom occupant densities and found comfort achievable up to 33.8 °C with air velocity near 1.2 m/s, with 80% acceptability between 23.4–29.7 °C operative temperature ([ScienceDirect — Status of thermal comfort in naturally ventilated classrooms, composite climate India](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132317305395); [ScienceDirect — thermal and acoustic comfort naturally ventilated university classrooms](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)).
- A four-month field study across 11 naturally ventilated Indian lecture halls with 828 student samples measured air speed at every seat and found noise up to 59.9 dBA was accepted at 28.8 °C, with productivity peaking around 27.8–29.8 °C operative temperature at ~56 dBA ([ScienceDirect, same study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)).
- Non-uniform fan-induced airflow across seat layouts (front row vs back row, under-fan vs between-fan seats) was specifically studied in Indian university classrooms in warm-humid climate ([ScienceDirect — non-uniform distribution of fan-induced air, warm humid India](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132323004006); [ScienceDirect — ceiling-fan-induced non-uniform thermal environment, seat layout](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132321006338)).
- Prevalence: naturally ventilated classrooms without AC are the overwhelming majority of Indian government and most private schools; ceiling fans are near-universal there. Prayer halls (mosques, gurdwaras, temples) and community/marriage halls almost always rely on multiple ceiling fans, often at high density, frequently with people seated on the floor (lower occupant height ≈0.3–0.6 m) rather than on chairs. **[UNVERIFIED]** quantitative national count of such spaces — no single dataset found.

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Thermal plume vs fan downwash (opposing flows).** Each occupant generates a buoyant convective plume rising at roughly 0.1–0.25 m/s near the body, strengthening with height above the head due to entrainment. The ceiling fan's downwash is a forced, denser momentum jet moving downward at the same location. These two flows are directly opposed in the breathing zone. CFD and manikin studies of ceiling-fan-driven airflow around human bodies confirm this interaction and show it depends on fan speed, body posture (seated/standing), and fan hover height ([ResearchGate — Effect of ceiling fan on human thermal comfort, air-conditioned space](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268572693_Effect_of_Using_Ceiling_Fan_on_Human_Thermal_Comfort_in_Air-Conditioned_Space); [ScienceDirect — manikin airflow DOAS + ceiling fans](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132322001470)).
- **Low-speed trapping effect (counter-intuitive, important for safety messaging).** At low fan speeds, the downward momentum of the jet can be just strong enough to cancel/oppose the occupant's own thermal plume without clearing it — this can trap exhaled aerosols and warm, CO₂-rich air at breathing height rather than dispersing them, which is worse for air-quality than either a higher fan speed or the fan being off **[reported finding, source quality moderate — summarized from secondary research synthesis; treat as UNVERIFIED pending primary citation]**. This has direct relevance to post-pandemic ventilation guidance for classrooms and halls.
- **Occupant bodies as bluff-body obstructions.** Rows of seated/standing people below the fan disk act as a porous, elevated obstruction layer. This (a) breaks up the floor-spreading jet into many smaller recirculation cells around individual bodies/furniture, (b) raises the effective "floor" the jet impinges on from ~0 m to ~0.4–1.2 m (chair/seated/standing height), shortening the usable throw distance similarly to the "ground effect" in Condition 22 (bed/desk) but distributed across the whole room rather than one object, and (c) increases turbulence intensity and wake-shedding noise at head height compared with an empty room.
- **Non-uniformity across the room.** Field studies explicitly document that air speed at seats directly under a fan can be 2–4× that of seats between fans or near walls/corners in the same classroom, producing comfort inequity row-to-row ([ScienceDirect — non-uniform fan-induced air, warm humid India](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132323004006)).
- **CO₂ / air-quality coupling.** Ceiling fans in a high-occupancy room do not add outdoor air; they only redistribute/mix the existing room air. In naturally ventilated classrooms this mixing can help bring fresh air from open windows/doors to the room center, but in sealed or low-infiltration halls, improved mixing does not reduce the absolute CO₂ load — it only prevents localized stagnant hot/CO₂-rich pockets. This distinction (ventilation vs mixing) is the standard point made in CBE Berkeley's air-movement literature ([CBE Guidebook on Fans for Cooling People](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/research/guidebook-on-fans-for-cooling-people/)) but was not separately quantified for a classroom CO₂ scenario in the sources reviewed — flagged **[UNVERIFIED — no India-specific CO2 measurement study located in this search]**.
- **Governing non-dimensional quantities:** same actuator-disk/momentum-theory and Richardson-number framework as other conditions, but with an added internal heat-source term distributed at occupant height rather than concentrated at the ceiling (cf. Condition 13 stratification) or at a wall (Condition 14). An "Airspeed Coverage Index" (ACI) has been proposed in the literature to combine fan speed, distance, and geometry effects into a predictor of occupant-level cooling effect in multi-fan, multi-occupant rooms ([escholarship — Building and Environment 2018 study](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2mk3n264/qt2mk3n264_noSplash_de02101012eb8ed43abfd4ebb4e2d627.pdf)).

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- Higher air speed directly raises the acceptable operative temperature: ASHRAE 55's elevated air-speed provision allows comfort up to roughly +3.5 °C above the still-air limit when clothing insulation is ~0.5 clo, now backed by the CBE Berkeley field-study database (>35,000 occupant responses) that found occupants generally *prefer more air movement* than codes/providers assume ([CBE elevated air speed and thermal comfort](https://cbe-berkeley.gitbook.io/fans-guidebook/full-guidebook/elevated-air-speed-and-thermal-comfort); [CBE Guidebook](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/research/guidebook-on-fans-for-cooling-people/)). The Indian classroom field study similarly found comfort extending to 33.8 °C at 1.2 m/s air speed ([ScienceDirect, composite climate India](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132317305395)).
- Noise is the binding constraint in classrooms/halls specifically because of occupant density + speech communication needs. The Indian lecture-hall study found 59.9 dBA accepted at 28.8 °C, and productivity actually peaked at a *lower* noise level (~56 dBA) with operative temperature 27.8–29.8 °C — i.e. there is a speed/noise/productivity trade-off specific to occupied teaching/worship spaces that does not exist in an empty bedroom ([ScienceDirect, same study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)).
- The "low speed can be worse than off" aerosol-trapping effect (Section 2) means that for air-quality/infection-control purposes in a packed room, the available evidence suggests avoiding a narrow band of low-but-nonzero speeds, running either off or at a speed high enough to fully break up the plume — **[UNVERIFIED — exact RPM band not found; depends on fan geometry, ceiling height and body spacing]**.
- Multi-fan synchronization: when several fans serve one hall, speed/phase coordination affects whether adjacent recirculation cells reinforce or fight each other (see Condition 08). No India-specific classroom data on synchronized vs independent speed control was found in this search.
- No evidence located of resonance/beat-frequency problems specific to occupancy (as distinct from the general blade-pass-frequency topic covered under other conditions); not flagged further here.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers (ranked for this condition)

1. **Lower-speed torque/airflow efficiency and low-noise blade design** — because classrooms/halls are frequently noise- and speech-intelligibility-constrained, a fan that delivers target air speed at occupant height at a *lower* RPM (via better airfoil/twist/chord design) directly relaxes the noise constraint identified in the Indian lecture-hall study.
2. **Wider, more uniform floor-footprint / lower core-to-edge velocity ratio** — reduces the row-to-row inequity documented in the non-uniform seat-layout studies ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132323004006), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132321006338)), i.e. favor broader, flatter velocity profiles over a narrow high-velocity core.
3. **Sweep diameter and blade count tuned to seating module/row spacing** rather than just room area, so fan footprints align with desk/seat rows (relevant for classroom and office furniture grids).
4. **Downrod length / clearance** set so the jet has enough throw to clear the "elevated occupant floor" (≈0.4–1.2 m) before spreading, analogous to Condition 22's ground-effect logic but averaged over a whole seated crowd rather than a single bed/desk.
5. **Winglets/tip treatment to control turbulence intensity at head height** — lower impact than 1–4 here but relevant for perceived "gustiness" in a crowd.

No India-specific ranked lever study for this exact condition was located; the ranking above is reasoned from the cited comfort/noise/non-uniformity studies rather than a single source, and should be treated as the team's working hypothesis, not a verified ranking. **[UNVERIFIED ranking]**

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No product targets row-to-row uniformity in multi-seat rooms.** All current BLDC fans are optimized/marketed for single-fan, single-room, no-occupant-crowd scenarios (Section 6). The documented seat-to-seat air-speed inequity in Indian classrooms ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132323004006)) is a real, measured, unaddressed gap.
- **Noise-vs-airflow trade-off is not tuned for speech-critical rooms.** Classrooms/halls need both comfort air speed and low enough noise for speech intelligibility; the Indian field study shows productivity is actually *lower* at the noise level needed for the top comfort temperature, implying current hardware forces a bad trade-off ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)). A quieter-per-CMM blade/motor design could relax this trade-off — estimated benefit **[UNVERIFIED — no quantified dB-vs-CMM tradeoff curve found for any commercial model]**.
- **No commercial multi-fan synchronization/coordination feature exists** for Indian residential-grade BLDC fans (unlike industrial HVLS fan farms). An app/IoT-based "hall mode" that phases or speed-matches several fans for uniform coverage is technically plausible given existing BLDC+WiFi platforms (e.g., Atomberg's app-controlled Halo) but not found in any current product.
- **No occupancy/CO₂-aware adaptive control exists** in the Indian market (the closest patent found, US 6,189,799, is single-fan and US-only). Given rising post-pandemic awareness of indoor air quality in Indian schools, a sensor-driven (occupancy count, CO₂, temperature) adaptive multi-fan control concept is a plausible differentiated concept — benefit would need primary validation, no quantified estimate available **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **The "low speed can trap aerosols" effect, if confirmed, is a safety/marketing-relevant unsolved problem** — current fans have no logic to avoid a "bad" low-speed band in occupied rooms. This requires primary-source verification before being used as a design claim.

**3–5 concept directions (team should validate before committing):**
1. **Uniformity-tuned blade/airfoil for broad, flat velocity profile** — mechanism: redesign twist/chord distribution to spread the core jet rather than concentrate it, reducing under-fan vs between-fan speed ratio. Expected benefit: more even seat-to-seat comfort (quantify via own CFD/field test — no number available yet). Risk: may reduce peak CMM/service-value score under BEE test protocol, which rewards concentrated-jet throughput, not uniformity. Cost: likely blade tooling change only. Novelty vs patents: appears open (no uniformity-specific patent found), but a dedicated patent search (Section 8 gap) is required before relying on this.
2. **Quiet-per-CMM motor/blade package aimed at speech-critical rooms** — mechanism: lower blade-tip noise and motor tonal noise at the RPM band needed for ~1.0–1.2 m/s occupant-level air speed. Expected benefit: shift the noise-vs-comfort trade-off curve favorably (unquantified). Risk: acoustic engineering cost; BEE/IS 374 doesn't currently reward low noise directly. Cost: moderate (bearing/motor quality, blade trailing edge). Novelty: likely low patent crowding specific to classrooms, but general low-noise-fan patents should be checked.
3. **App-coordinated multi-fan "hall mode"** — mechanism: BLDC fans with existing WiFi platforms synchronize speed/phase for even hall-wide coverage. Expected benefit: reduce competing-recirculation dead zones (Condition 08 physics). Risk: requires multiple fans from the same ecosystem; limited addressable market per hall. Cost: software/firmware, minimal hardware change. Novelty: single-fan occupancy patent exists (US 6,189,799); multi-fan synchronized version likely differentiable but needs search.
4. **Occupancy/CO₂-adaptive speed control** — mechanism: onboard or networked CO₂/occupancy sensing adjusts fan speed to avoid the low-speed aerosol-trapping band and to boost air movement when density/heat load rises. Expected benefit: air-quality/comfort improvement, currently unquantified, contingent on verifying the underlying aerosol-trapping physics first. Risk: sensor cost, false-positive/negative occupancy detection. Cost: added BOM for sensors. Novelty: moderate overlap with US 6,189,799 concept; needs design-around.
5. **Elevated-jet throw design for seated/standing crowds** (taller effective "ground clearance" before jet breakup, analogous to Condition 22 ground-effect logic but tuned to ~0.4–1.2 m occupant height rather than 0 m floor) — mechanism: adjust downrod/clearance and blade pitch so the jet's peak velocity zone lands at head/torso height across a crowd rather than at floor level. Expected benefit: unquantified; plausible from the physics in Section 2. Risk: may reduce whole-room circulation if jet is too focused on one height band. Cost: minimal (geometry only).

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Real-room test:** Recreate a packed classroom/hall mock-up (e.g., 30+ seated subjects or heated manikins/space heaters as human-heat-load surrogates at ~70–100 W each) under 1–3 ceiling fans. Grid of vane or hot-wire/hot-sphere anemometers at seated head height (~1.1 m) and standing/floor-seated height (~0.3–0.6 m, relevant for prayer-hall floor seating) across every "seat," following the seat-by-seat measurement protocol used in the Indian lecture-hall field study ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)).
- **Metrics:** air speed (mean + turbulence intensity) at every grid point; sound level (dB(A)) at seated-head height; power draw; and, if feasible, CO₂ decay/mixing time with a tracer gas release to separately quantify "ventilation" vs "mixing" effects (Section 2).
- **Smoke/fog visualization** around a thermal manikin or heater at varying fan speeds to directly observe the plume-vs-downwash interaction and look for the low-speed "trapping" band described in Section 2–3 — this is the most direct way to verify or refute that **[UNVERIFIED]** claim cheaply.
- **CFD:** blade-resolved transient (sliding mesh) or MRF simulation of the fan(s) with multiple thermal-manikin or simplified heated-cylinder occupant models distributed at realistic classroom/hall density; RANS k-ω SST or LES; validate against the above real-room grid measurements before trusting room-scale predictions. Compare against the escholarship ACI framework ([link](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2mk3n264/qt2mk3n264_noSplash_de02101012eb8ed43abfd4ebb4e2d627.pdf)) as a cross-check metric.

## 12. Summary

High-occupancy Indian spaces — classrooms, offices, prayer halls, community halls — turn ceiling-fan design into a multi-occupant thermal-plume, noise, and air-quality problem, not just a single-room airflow problem. Peer-reviewed Indian field studies document real seat-to-seat comfort inequity and a genuine noise-vs-comfort trade-off in lecture halls, and the broader literature shows occupants generally want more air movement than is typically provided, with a plausible (but unverified in this pass) risk that low fan speeds can trap exhaled aerosols at breathing height by opposing the body's thermal plume. No Indian or global ceiling-fan brand surveyed markets a product or feature explicitly designed for this condition, and the patent landscape search here turned up only one loosely relevant single-fan occupancy-control patent — both are gaps, not just dead ends.

**Top 5 insights:**
1. Measured seat-to-seat air-speed non-uniformity in Indian classrooms is a real, sourced, currently unaddressed design problem ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132323004006)).
2. There is a quantified noise-vs-comfort-vs-productivity trade-off specific to occupied teaching/worship spaces (productivity peaked at *lower* noise/temperature than the comfort maximum) ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360132324010540)).
3. Occupants broadly want *more*, not less, air movement than codes assume (CBE Berkeley / ASHRAE 55 database) ([CBE](https://cbe.berkeley.edu/research/guidebook-on-fans-for-cooling-people/)).
4. A possible low-speed aerosol-trapping effect (plume opposition) is a high-value but unverified claim that should be the team's first wind-tunnel/smoke-test priority.
5. The market and patent landscape both show a real white space for occupancy-aware or hall-synchronized multi-fan products — no strong blocking IP was found, but the patent search here was shallow and must be redone properly (Section 8) before committing engineering resources.

**Open questions / not found in this pass:**
- Primary peer-reviewed source for the low-speed aerosol-trapping claim.
- Any India-specific CO₂/ventilation-vs-mixing quantitative study for classrooms or prayer halls.
- A proper 10–20-family patent landscape for occupancy-adaptive or multi-fan-synchronized control (only 2 patents surfaced, both thin leads).
- Primary datasheets (not aggregator articles) for Crompton, Havells, Orient, and Usha models cited in Section 6.
- Any quantitative study of ceiling-fan behavior in floor-seated prayer-hall contexts specifically (all seat-layout studies found used chair/desk seating).
