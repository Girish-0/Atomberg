# Condition 26 — Voltage Fluctuation, Low Voltage and Inverter/Solar Backup

*AtomQuest 2026 evidence dossier — ceiling fan aerodynamics under 160–270 V supply, inverter (square/modified-sine) backup, and solar DC operation.*

## 1. Condition definition

- **Scope**: Indian mains is nominally 230 V ± 10% (207–253 V) per IS 12360, but real-world supply commonly swings 160–270 V, with brownouts to 140–180 V in rural feeders and peri-urban areas during peak load [UNVERIFIED — no single authoritative field-measurement citation found in this pass]. IS 374:2019 (BIS specification for electric ceiling fans) covers single-phase, 50 Hz, induction or BLDC fans up to 250 V rated voltage and ≤125 W input, and requires manufacturers to mark rated voltage/voltage range on the fan ([BIS IS 374:2019, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gov.in.is.374.2019); [BIS PDF](https://bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/PM_IS-374-final_compressed.pdf)).
- **Backup scenarios**: (a) home inverter/UPS supplying square-wave or modified-sine-wave AC during outages, (b) solar home systems feeding 12 V/24 V DC directly to DC fans, bypassing inversion entirely, (c) rural low-voltage grid tails where the local distribution transformer secondary sags 20–25% below nominal under load, which is documented to cause higher iron losses and potential permanent damage to induction-motor appliances ([pManifold, "Low Voltage DC Fans"](https://www.pmanifold.com/low-voltage-dc-fans-bringing-scale-to-solving-energy-efficiency-and-access-problems-in-india/)).
- **Prevalence in India**: Voltage instability is described as "a daily reality" in semi-urban and rural distribution networks; off-grid and weak-grid rural households show markedly different (seasonal, outage-prone) electricity-consumption patterns ([ScienceDirect, "Weathering electricity demand"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214629620300219)). Exact % of Indian households facing sub-180 V conditions regularly is [UNVERIFIED] — no CEA/state-DISCOM dataset was retrieved in this pass; this is a gap to close with primary utility data (CEA Power Supply Position reports, state DISCOM AT&C-loss/voltage-quality filings).

## 2. Aerodynamic physics

- **Induction motor**: Electromagnetic torque scales with the square of applied voltage (T ∝ V²); a 10% undervoltage produces roughly 19% torque loss while speed itself drops only ~1.5%, because a fan's torque–speed load curve is forgiving at reduced speed (fan/centrifugal loads have cube-law torque-vs-speed demand, not constant-torque demand) ([Industrial Monitor Direct](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/ac-induction-motor-voltage-tolerance-performance-effects-at-10-deviation); [eng-tips thread](https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/fan-speed-and-voltage.421331/)). Practically: an induction-motor ceiling fan's rotational speed sags only modestly as voltage drops to ~180–200 V, but available torque margin shrinks sharply, so under load (dust buildup, bearing drag, high ambient temperature raising winding resistance) the fan can stall or hum without spinning — a classic low-voltage induction-fan failure mode reported in the field ([pManifold](https://www.pmanifold.com/low-voltage-dc-fans-bringing-scale-to-solving-energy-efficiency-and-access-problems-in-india/)).
- **Consequence for the aerodynamic envelope**: because thrust/airflow in actuator-disk terms scales with blade tip speed squared (T ∝ Ω²D⁴ρ, non-dimensionally via thrust coefficient C_T), even the modest RPM droop from undervoltage compounds into a measurable air-delivery and velocity-at-occupant-height loss; the blade operates at a lower local Reynolds number and lower angle-of-attack-driven lift until speed stabilizes. Quantified %, test data: [UNVERIFIED] — no peer-reviewed or BIS lab dataset specifically quantifying m³/min loss vs. supply voltage for Indian ceiling fans was found in this pass.
- **BLDC motor + electronic controller**: A BLDC ceiling fan's driver regulates an internal DC bus (commonly ~24 V in commercial reference designs, see §4) independent of line voltage, so motor speed and hence blade RPM/airflow stay essentially constant across the rated AC input window. Commercial claims report ±1% speed regulation across the full input range in a reference design ([TI TIDA-00386](https://www.ti.com/tool/TIDA-00386)), and marketing material for consumer BLDC fans states consistent airflow performance "from 90 V to 300 V" ([Crompton product literature, aggregated via desidime/smarthousegears search](https://smarthousegears.com/articles/best-bldc-ceiling-fans-india)) — this last figure is a manufacturer claim, not an independently verified lab test, and should be marked **[UNVERIFIED/MARKETING]**.
- **Inverter waveform effects**: A modified-sine-wave inverter output is the sum of two phase-shifted square waves (zero/+peak/zero/−peak/zero steps), which inherently carries high total harmonic distortion — commonly cited as THD > 15%, versus a grid/utility target of <3% ([Edecoa, "Harmonics in Inverter Systems"](https://www.edecoa.com/blogs/technical-resources/harmonics-in-inverter-systems); [epanorama.net](https://epanorama.net/newepa/2020/12/13/modified-sine-wave-inverters)). For an induction-motor fan, these harmonics add core/eddy-current losses, cause magnetic-vibration-driven audible humming, and distort the torque-speed characteristic relative to sinusoidal supply ([Quora engineering discussion aggregation](https://www.quora.com/Is-there-any-way-or-a-hack-to-reduce-the-hum-in-a-modified-sine-wave-inverter-What-will-happen-if-we-introduce-an-alternator)). A BLDC fan's front-end rectifier/PFC stage is comparatively insensitive to waveform shape (it rectifies to DC regardless), so aerodynamic output is more stable on inverter power, though the rectifier ripple and EMI behavior on a non-sinusoidal input are themselves [UNVERIFIED] for consumer BLDC fan ICs specifically — most published data is for the AC-mains case, not modified-sine inverter input.
- **Non-dimensional framing for the team**: track fan tip Reynolds number Re = ΩR·c/ν (chord c, tip radius R), and treat supply-voltage droop as an input-torque/Ω perturbation on the standard actuator-disk thrust relation T = 2ρA(ΩR)²C_T, rather than a separate aerodynamic regime — the physics inside the room (downwash, wall return, recirculation) is unchanged in kind, only in magnitude, once RPM is restored.

## 3. Role of speed / RPM / frequency

- **Induction motor**: speed is locked to line frequency (50 Hz, synchronous speed minus slip) and is largely *frequency*-dependent, not voltage-dependent, for an unregulated shaded-pole/capacitor-start fan motor — voltage drop mainly erodes torque margin and raises slip, not frequency. Indian inverters generally hold 50 Hz ± tight tolerance, so classical induction fans see little speed change on inverter power; what changes is torque reserve (per §2) and acoustic/thermal behavior from harmonics.
- **BLDC/inverter-drive fans**: speed is set electronically (PWM commutation frequency derived from a reference clock inside the controller, e.g. MSP430-class MCU in the TI reference design), decoupled from the 50 Hz line — so BLDC fans are inherently more tolerant of frequency wobble on cheap inverters, and can hold a chosen RPM band regardless of input waveform, as long as the rectified DC bus stays within the controller's regulation window (90–265 V AC in the TI design) ([TI TIDA-00386 user guide](https://www.ti.com/lit/ug/tiduaj0a/tiduaj0a.pdf)).
- **Resonance / beat-frequency / flicker**: no India-specific published data found on beat-frequency or blade-pass-tone issues arising specifically from inverter-driven ceiling fans [UNVERIFIED — open question]; however, harmonic-induced magnetic vibration in induction motors on non-sinusoidal supply is a known general EE phenomenon (§2) and could plausibly couple into blade-pass acoustic tones; this is a testable hypothesis, not yet evidenced for this exact product class.
- **Variable speed / reverse / "natural breeze" modes**: no sourced evidence found that any of these modes specifically mitigate voltage-fluctuation or inverter-waveform effects; they are independent feature sets (addressed in other condition briefs, e.g. #13 stratification, #27 low-speed). Mark as **[UNVERIFIED]** for this condition specifically — plausible synergy (e.g., controller-side voltage compensation logic could in principle be combined with natural-breeze pulsing) but no product or paper found demonstrating it.

## 4. Blade and fan geometry levers

Voltage/inverter robustness is overwhelmingly an **electronics/motor-controller** problem, not a blade-geometry problem — the governing lever is the drive architecture, not sweep, chord, or twist. Ranked relevance to this specific condition:

1. **Motor topology (BLDC vs. induction) and controller design** — dominant lever. A well-designed BLDC controller (rectifier + PFC + regulated DC bus + closed-loop commutation) decouples blade RPM from line-voltage swings almost entirely; this is an electronics choice, not an aerodynamic one.
2. **DC bus regulation range and component voltage rating** — determines the usable AC window (90–265 V in the TI reference design; some consumer models claim 90–300 V — [UNVERIFIED commercial claim]).
3. **Blade moment of inertia / rotor inertia** — a heavier rotor+blade assembly resists transient RPM dips during brief sags/surges better (flywheel effect), at a cost of slower start-up and more current draw on starting under low voltage; a genuine design trade-off but not evidenced quantitatively for Indian products in this pass [UNVERIFIED].
4. **Blade sweep, chord, airfoil, twist, tip shape** — essentially unaffected by this condition; these levers matter for the *steady-state* aerodynamic performance once RPM is achieved, not for voltage robustness itself. They are addressed by the other 27 condition briefs.
5. **Downrod/clearance/canopy** — not relevant to this condition.

## 5. Materials and manufacturing

- Not a primary driver for this condition. The relevant "material" consideration is electronic: component voltage derating (MOSFETs, electrolytic capacitors, varistors/MOVs for surge protection) must be rated for the full expected swing plus transient surges (e.g., inverter switching spikes, grid restoration surges after an outage). No India-specific component-cost or BOM data was found in this pass [UNVERIFIED].
- Cost implication: wide-voltage-range BLDC drive electronics (flyback AC-DC stage, PFC, dedicated motor-control IC such as TI's DRV10983) add BOM cost versus a simple capacitor-run induction motor, which is reflected in BLDC fans' higher retail price (₹2,000–4,000+ vs. ₹1,000–1,800 for basic induction fans in the Indian market) — general market observation, exact INR cost breakdown [UNVERIFIED].

## 9. Unsolved gaps and opportunities

- **No independent, India-specific, quantified test data** exists (that this pass could find) comparing actual air-delivery/RPM stability of induction vs. BLDC ceiling fans across the real 160–270 V field distribution, as opposed to manufacturer marketing claims of "90–300 V." This is the single biggest evidence gap for the hackathon team to either close with original bench testing or explicitly flag as unverified in their own deck.
- **Inverter-waveform-specific testing is essentially absent** in public literature for *ceiling fans specifically* (most harmonics/motor-hum literature is generic industrial-motor literature, not ceiling-fan-specific). A team could differentiate by publishing the first such comparison (induction vs. BLDC fan on pure sine vs. modified-sine vs. square-wave inverter) — low cost, high credibility value.
- **Rotor-inertia-as-buffer** against transient voltage sags (brief dips during motor starts elsewhere in the house, grid switching transients) is a plausible, cheap mechanical lever that appears undiscussed in the located literature — worth a feasibility check (added inertia cost in material/INR vs. measured RPM-droop damping).
- **Solar/DC-direct fans remain a separate, non-integrated product category** rather than a dual-mode (AC mains + DC solar input) design; a fan that accepts both 230 V AC *and* 12/24 V DC directly (no inverter round-trip loss) could be a genuine novel angle, especially for rural/off-grid-adjacent markets — needs patent-landscape confirmation (not checked in this pass) and a feasibility read on dual-input power-supply topology cost.
- Concept directions (unranked, benefit estimates withheld pending real data):
  1. Dual-input (AC/DC) power stage — mechanism: single power board accepts both 230 V AC and 12–48 V DC bus, auto-detects source; benefit: eliminates inverter conversion loss for solar users [benefit % UNVERIFIED]; risk: added BOM/certification complexity; novelty vs. patents: needs InPASS check.
  2. Firmware-level "grid-quality" compensation (THD/sag detection) with an active response (soft RPM hold vs. let-it-droop) — mechanism: controller monitors rectified bus ripple as a proxy for input waveform quality and chooses a compensation strategy; benefit [UNVERIFIED]; risk: firmware IP overlap with existing BEMF/compensation patents (§8); cost impact: software-only, low marginal BOM.
  3. Added rotor/blade-hub inertia tuned for sag-ride-through — mechanism: mechanical flywheel effect; benefit [UNVERIFIED, needs bench test]; risk: slower start-up, higher starting current; cost: minor material increase.
  4. Publish an open, India-specific voltage-robustness benchmark (test rig + published CMM-vs-voltage curves for major brands) — mechanism: pure measurement/PR play, not a product feature; benefit: market differentiation and credibility; risk: reputational if numbers are unfavorable to Atomberg itself; cost: bench-test time only.

## 10. Measurement and validation plan

- **Rig**: a single ceiling fan (test both an induction-motor and a BLDC sample) on an adjustable AC supply (variac or programmable AC source capable of 140–270 V at 50 Hz, plus a second run through a low-cost modified-sine-wave inverter and a pure-sine inverter for comparison).
- **Instruments**: vane or hot-wire anemometer on a fixed grid beneath the fan (standard BIS/BEE air-delivery test geometry per IS 374:2019 test method — confirm exact grid spacing from the standard itself), a true-RMS power meter (W, PF, THD) at the fan's input, a sound level meter (dB(A)) at a fixed distance, and a tachometer/strobe for RPM.
- **Protocol**: step the supply voltage in ~10 V increments from 270 V down to 140 V (and back up, to check hysteresis/restart behavior) at each of the fan's speed settings; repeat the full sweep on modified-sine and pure-sine inverter sources; log RPM, air delivery (CMM), power (W), THD, and dB(A) at each point.
- **Metrics to report**: CMM vs. voltage curve, service value (CMM/W) vs. voltage curve, RPM stability band, any stall/restart voltage threshold, THD-vs-noise correlation.
- **CFD**: not the primary validation tool for this condition (it's an electrical/control problem); if used at all, model the RPM-vs-time transient measured on the bench as a time-varying rotational boundary condition in an existing validated room-airflow CFD setup, and compare resulting occupant-height velocity transients against the bench anemometer-grid data.

## 12. Summary

**Executive summary**: Voltage fluctuation and inverter/solar backup is fundamentally an electronics/motor-control problem rather than a blade-aerodynamics problem: induction motors lose torque margin roughly with the square of voltage drop (risking stall/hum under real household load) while BLDC fans with a regulated DC bus and closed-loop commutation (demonstrated concretely in TI's 90–265 Vac / 24 Vdc ±1% reference design) hold RPM — and therefore aerodynamic output — essentially constant across the voltage window their power stage is designed for; modified-sine inverters add harmonic distortion that stresses induction motors with extra heating/noise but has little effect on a properly rectified BLDC front end. Indian brands (Crompton, Superfan, Havells, Atomberg) all market wide-voltage BLDC ranges (commonly cited as 90–300 V), but these are marketing claims not independently verified in this research pass, and no India-specific bench dataset quantifying real-world air-delivery stability vs. supply voltage was located — this is the dossier's single biggest evidence gap and the clearest low-cost opportunity (an original bench test) for the hackathon team.

**Top 5 insights**:
1. This condition is won or lost in the power-electronics/motor-control layer, not in blade geometry — blade-lever work (sections 4–5 of the framework) is largely orthogonal to this specific brief.
2. Induction-motor torque falls with V² while speed falls only slightly — the real failure mode is stall/overheat under load, not gentle slowdown.
3. BLDC + regulated DC bus (TI's 90–265 Vac → 24 Vdc ±1% design is the best-documented primary-source example found) is the proven architecture for voltage robustness; "90–300 V" marketing claims from Indian brands need independent verification.
4. Modified-sine-wave inverters carry >15% THD vs. <3% grid target, stressing induction motors with extra heat/noise; BLDC fans are comparatively insensitive because of the rectified DC-bus front end.
5. A dual AC/DC-input power stage and a published, independent voltage-robustness benchmark are both plausible, low-cost, currently-unoccupied differentiation angles — neither was found implemented or tested in any source located during this pass.

**Open questions / not found in this pass**:
- No CEA/DISCOM dataset quantifying what fraction of Indian households regularly see sub-180 V or above 270 V.
- No peer-reviewed or BIS-lab quantified curve of CMM/RPM vs. supply voltage for any specific Indian ceiling fan model.
- No study of beat-frequency, blade-pass tonal noise, or resonance specifically triggered by inverter harmonics on a ceiling fan.
- Full-text findings of the ScienceDirect Zeta-converter paper and the IEEE 9641617 "High Voltage BLDC ceiling fan Regulator" paper were not read in depth in this pass — flagged for follow-up.
- A full 10–20-family patent table (InPASS-specific search) was not completed; only 7 families were surfaced via the general searches run here.
